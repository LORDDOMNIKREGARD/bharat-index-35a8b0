Section 01

Stock Market Basics

What is a Stock Market?

A stock market is a marketplace where buyers and sellers trade ownership stakes in companies. Think of it like a giant auction house — but instead of paintings or antiques, people are buying and selling small pieces of businesses.

A stock market is like a mandi (vegetable market) — but instead of vegetables, companies are the "products," and instead of farmers, it's businesses selling slices of themselves to the public.
What is a stock market?
A stock market is an organised platform where publicly listed companies' shares are bought and sold. It helps companies raise capital from the public and provides investors a chance to participate in corporate growth. In India, the primary stock markets are the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) and the National Stock Exchange (NSE).

Why Do Stock Markets Exist?

Stock markets exist to solve a fundamental problem: companies need money to grow, but individuals have idle savings that need to work harder than a bank FD. The market brings these two needs together.

For companies: Raise large sums without taking loans. Tata Motors doesn't need to beg a bank — it can sell shares to millions of people.
For investors: Grow wealth by sharing in corporate profits. Rather than putting ₹1 lakh in a fixed deposit at 6%, an investor can buy shares of a growing company and potentially earn 15–20% annually.

What is the difference between primary and secondary markets?
The primary market is where a company first sells its securities to the public (like an IPO), directly raising capital. The secondary market is the stock exchange where investors trade these securities amongst themselves — the company doesn't receive this money. Think of it as: primary market is a brand-new car dealership, secondary market is the used-car market.

IPO and FPO

IPO (Initial Public Offering): The first time a private company sells its shares to the public. This is how a company "goes public."

Real Example
Zomato's IPO in July 2021 raised ₹9,375 crore. The issue price was ₹76 per share. Millions of retail investors applied, and the IPO was subscribed 38 times — meaning people wanted 38x more shares than were available.

FPO (Follow-on Public Offering): When an already-listed company issues more shares to the public. The company already has shares in the market but needs more capital.

What is an IPO? Why would a company do an IPO?
An IPO, or Initial Public Offering, is when a private company sells its shares to the general public for the first time, listing itself on a stock exchange. Companies do IPOs to: (1) raise large capital without debt, (2) provide an exit route for early investors and promoters, (3) increase brand credibility and visibility, and (4) use shares as currency for future acquisitions. After an IPO, the company's shares trade freely on the stock exchange.

What is a Share?

A share is a unit of ownership in a company. If a company has issued 100 shares and you own 10, you own 10% of the company. You are entitled to 10% of all profits distributed (dividends) and 10% of the assets if the company is wound up.

Imagine a pizza cut into 8 slices. Each slice is a share. If you own 2 slices, you own 2/8 = 25% of the pizza. The bigger the pizza gets, the more valuable your 2 slices.

What is market capitalisation? What is the difference between large-cap and small-cap?
Market capitalisation is the total market value of all outstanding shares of a company, calculated as share price multiplied by number of shares. Large-cap companies (like Reliance, TCS) have market caps above ₹20,000 crore — they are established, stable, and less volatile. Small-cap companies are smaller, more volatile, riskier, but may offer higher growth potential. SEBI classifies the top 100 companies by market cap as large-cap, 101–250 as mid-cap, and the rest as small-cap.

Bull Market vs Bear Market

Bull Market: A period of rising stock prices, typically 20%+ rise from recent lows, accompanied by economic optimism. A bull "charges forward and upward."
Bear Market: A period of falling stock prices, typically 20%+ decline from recent highs, accompanied by pessimism. A bear "swipes downward with its paw."

Real Example
India experienced a massive bull market from 2020 lows (Sensex at ~25,000) to the 2024 highs (~85,000). The COVID crash of March 2020 was a bear market — Sensex fell ~40% in weeks.
What is the difference between a bull market and a bear market?
A bull market is a period of sustained price increases — generally defined as a 20%+ rise — driven by economic optimism, strong corporate earnings, and investor confidence. A bear market is the opposite: a 20%+ sustained decline, driven by economic slowdowns, poor earnings, or external shocks. The terms come from how each animal attacks — a bull thrusts upward, a bear swipes downward.

Dividends, Bonus Shares, Stock Splits, Rights Issue, Buybacks

Dividend: A share of profits paid to shareholders, usually as a fixed amount per share. Example: "Infosys declares ₹20 dividend per share."

Bonus Shares: Free additional shares given to existing shareholders. Example: "1:1 bonus" means for every share you hold, you get one more free. Your total shares double but share price halves — so net value stays same.

Stock Split: Dividing each share into multiple shares. Example: A 2:1 split of a ₹1,000 share gives you 2 shares at ₹500 each. Done to improve affordability and liquidity.

Rights Issue: A company offers additional shares to existing shareholders at a discounted price, before offering to the public. Shareholders have the "right" to buy more.

Buyback: A company buys back its own shares from the market, reducing total shares outstanding. This increases EPS and signals that management thinks the stock is undervalued.

What is a stock split? Why would a company do it?
A stock split divides existing shares into multiple shares. For example, in a 10:1 split, each ₹1,000 share becomes 10 shares of ₹100 each. The company's total market cap remains the same, but the price per share drops. Companies do splits primarily to improve the stock's liquidity — a lower price makes the stock accessible to more retail investors, increasing trading volume. MRF, priced above ₹1 lakh per share, is a famous example of a company that has never split its shares.

Sensex & Nifty

What is Sensex?

Sensex stands for Sensitive Index. It is the benchmark index of the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE), comprising the 30 largest and most actively traded stocks on the BSE.

History: Sensex was launched on January 1, 1986, with a base value of 100. The base year was 1978-79. It crossed 1,000 for the first time in 1990, 10,000 in 2006, and 80,000 in 2024.

Key Formula: Sensex is calculated using the Free-Float Market Capitalisation method. The index value = (Current Free-Float Market Cap of 30 stocks / Base Market Cap) × Base Index Value (100)
What is Sensex?
Sensex, short for Sensitive Index, is the benchmark stock index of the Bombay Stock Exchange. It tracks the performance of 30 financially strong and well-established companies across various sectors of the Indian economy. Launched in 1986 with a base of 100, it is calculated using the free-float market capitalisation method. When Sensex rises, it generally means the overall market is performing well. It serves as a barometer of India's economic health.

What is Nifty 50?

Nifty 50 is the benchmark index of the National Stock Exchange (NSE), comprising the 50 largest and most liquid stocks listed on the NSE.

History: Nifty was launched on April 22, 1996, with a base of 1,000 (base year: November 3, 1995). "Nifty" = NSE + Fifty. It crossed 10,000 in 2017, 20,000 in 2023, and 26,000 in 2024.

What is Nifty 50?
Nifty 50 is the flagship benchmark index of the National Stock Exchange of India. It represents the weighted average of 50 of India's largest and most liquid companies across 13 sectors. Managed by NSE Indices Limited, it serves as the standard for tracking Indian equity market performance. The name "Nifty" combines "NSE" and "Fifty." Financial products like index mutual funds, ETFs, and F&O contracts are built around Nifty.

Free-Float Market Capitalisation

Not all shares of a company are available for public trading. Promoters (founders), government, and strategic investors hold shares they rarely sell. Free-float refers to shares actually available to the general public for trading.

Free-Float Market Cap = Share Price × Number of Freely Tradeable Shares
Example
Suppose a company has 100 crore shares. The promoter holds 60 crore shares (not traded). Only 40 crore shares float freely in the market. If price = ₹500:
Total Market Cap = 100 crore × ₹500 = ₹50,000 crore
Free-Float Market Cap = 40 crore × ₹500 = ₹20,000 crore

Why Reliance, HDFC Bank, Infosys, TCS Dominate Indices

Indices are weighted by free-float market capitalisation. These companies have enormous market caps and good free-float percentages, so their weight in the index is high. If Reliance has a 12% weight in Nifty, a 5% rise in Reliance alone will push Nifty up by 0.6%. This is why market watchers track these "heavyweight" stocks closely.

Top Nifty Weightages (approximate): Reliance Industries (~12%), HDFC Bank (~12%), ICICI Bank (~8%), Infosys (~7%), TCS (~6%), Bajaj Finance (~5%). Together, just these 5-6 companies can move the index significantly.

Section 03

Stock Exchanges

BSE — Bombay Stock Exchange

Founded in 1875, BSE is Asia's oldest stock exchange, located on Dalal Street, Mumbai. Originally called the "Native Share & Stock Brokers Association." It has over 5,000 listed companies — more than any exchange in the world by number of listings.

BSE's market index is Sensex. BSE trading was initially physical (open outcry), transitioning to electronic trading in 1995.

NSE — National Stock Exchange

Founded in 1992, the NSE was created by the Government of India to introduce fully electronic trading and increase transparency. Before NSE, stock trading was manual, opaque, and prone to manipulation.

NSE introduced screen-based, order-matching trading — removing the need for physical presence on a trading floor. By 2000, NSE became the world's largest stock exchange by number of trades. Today, NSE handles ~90% of India's equity trading volume. Index: Nifty 50.

Why was NSE created? Why is it more popular than BSE in trading volume?
NSE was established in 1992 by the Government of India to reform the stock market — introducing transparency, electronic trading, and standardised settlement. BSE was manual, dominated by brokers, and prone to manipulation. NSE's fully computerised order-matching system gave every investor, regardless of location, equal access. Today, NSE handles around 90% of India's equity trading volume because of its superior technology, tighter spreads, and being the primary venue for F&O trading (futures and options on Nifty).

Role of SEBI

SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) is the regulator of capital markets, established in 1988 and given statutory powers in 1992 via the SEBI Act.

SEBI's key functions:

  • Protects investor interests
  • Regulates and develops the securities market
  • Prevents insider trading, price manipulation, and fraud
  • Registers and regulates stockbrokers, mutual funds, FIIs
  • Approves IPOs (companies must file offer documents with SEBI)
  • Imposes penalties on rule violators
SEBI is to the stock market what the RBI is to banks — it makes the rules, supervises players, and punishes cheaters.
What is SEBI? Why is it important?
SEBI stands for Securities and Exchange Board of India, established under the SEBI Act of 1992. It is the apex regulator for India's capital markets. SEBI protects investors from fraud, ensures transparent and fair markets, regulates market intermediaries like brokers and mutual funds, and approves IPO applications. For example, before Zomato could list on NSE, it had to file a DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI. SEBI has the power to investigate and impose fines — it has penalised companies for insider trading and market manipulation.
Section 04

Market Participants

Key Market Participants

Retail Investors: Individuals investing their own savings. They form the largest group by number but not by value.

Institutional Investors: Large organisations investing on behalf of others — mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds.

FIIs (Foreign Institutional Investors) / FPIs (Foreign Portfolio Investors): Foreign entities investing in Indian markets — JPMorgan, BlackRock, Goldman Sachs. When FIIs buy, markets tend to rise. When they sell ("FII outflow"), markets fall.

DIIs (Domestic Institutional Investors): Indian institutions — LIC, SBI Mutual Fund, HDFC Mutual Fund. Often they buy when FIIs sell, providing market stability.

Mutual Funds: Pool money from thousands of investors and deploy it professionally across stocks and bonds. SIP inflows have made Indian mutual funds powerful market participants.

Market Makers: Entities that always quote a buy and sell price for a security, ensuring liquidity even when trading is thin.

Brokers: Intermediaries who execute buy/sell orders on behalf of investors. Zerodha, Groww, Upstox, HDFC Securities are popular Indian brokers.

Depository Participants (DPs): Agents of NSDL or CDSL who maintain your demat account. Banks like SBI, HDFC, ICICI and brokers like Zerodha act as DPs.

What are FIIs and DIIs? How do they affect the market?
FIIs (Foreign Institutional Investors) or FPIs (Foreign Portfolio Investors) are foreign entities — like global funds, insurance companies, and hedge funds — that invest in Indian securities. When FIIs bring in dollars to buy Indian stocks, demand rises and markets go up. When they withdraw (FII outflow), markets fall and the rupee also weakens. DIIs (Domestic Institutional Investors) are Indian institutions like LIC, mutual funds, and pension funds. They often counterbalance FII selling by buying during dips, providing market stability. This FII-DII dynamic is closely watched by market analysts daily.
Section 05

Demat & Trading

What is a Demat Account?

Demat stands for Dematerialised. Before demat, shares existed as physical paper certificates — which could be forged, lost, or stolen. Today, all shares are held electronically in a demat account, like how money is stored digitally in a bank account.

A demat account is like a bank account for shares. Just as a bank account holds your money electronically, a demat account holds your stocks, bonds, and mutual fund units electronically.

Demat vs Trading Account: A demat account stores your securities. A trading account is used to place buy/sell orders. You need both — the trading account is the door, the demat account is the storage room.

Why do we need a demat account? Why can't stocks simply be stored like cash?
Before demat accounts existed, shares were physical paper certificates — cumbersome to transfer, prone to forgery, loss, and theft. The 1992 Harshad Mehta scam partly exploited this paper-based system. SEBI mandated dematerialisation in 1996. Now, shares are held electronically in demat accounts maintained by depositories (NSDL/CDSL). Cash can be stored physically because it's a fungible commodity, but shares are specific ownership records that must be precisely tracked and transferred — making electronic storage essential for accuracy, speed, and security.

NSDL and CDSL

NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited): Established in 1996, promoted by NSE. India's first depository. Holds the majority of demat accounts by value.
CDSL (Central Depository Services Limited): Established in 1999, promoted by BSE. More demat accounts by number. CDSL is a publicly listed company on BSE.

Settlement Cycle: After you buy shares, you receive them in your demat account in T+1 days (T = trade day). India moved from T+2 to T+1 settlement in 2023, becoming one of the fastest in the world.

Bonds & Fixed Income

What is a Bond?

A bond is a debt instrument — when you buy a bond, you are lending money to the issuer (government or company) who promises to pay you regular interest (called coupon) and return your principal at maturity.

A bond is like giving your friend a formal loan. "I'll give you ₹1,000 today. You pay me ₹80 every year for 5 years, and return my ₹1,000 at the end." This ₹80 is the coupon, ₹1,000 is the face value (principal), and 5 years is the maturity period.

Stocks vs Bonds: Stocks give ownership; bonds give creditor status. Stockholders are owners; bondholders are lenders. In case of bankruptcy, bondholders are paid before stockholders.

Why would anyone buy a bond instead of a stock?
Bonds offer predictable, fixed income — ideal for risk-averse investors like retirees or institutions. While stocks can give higher returns, they are volatile and don't guarantee income. Government bonds (G-Secs) are essentially risk-free since the government can always print money to repay. Bonds also provide portfolio diversification — when stocks fall during recessions, bonds often rise (investors flee to safety). Insurance companies and pension funds are legally required to hold large proportions of bonds.

Types of Bonds

Government Securities (G-Secs): Bonds issued by the Government of India. Completely safe — the government can always print money. Used by RBI for monetary policy (Open Market Operations).

Treasury Bills (T-Bills): Short-term government debt (91 days, 182 days, 364 days). Issued at a discount and redeemed at face value. No coupon — the profit is the difference between issue price and face value.

Corporate Bonds: Issued by companies. Higher coupon than government bonds (to compensate for higher risk). Rated by agencies like CRISIL, CARE, ICRA.

Zero Coupon Bonds: Pay no periodic interest. Sold at a deep discount and redeemed at face value. The return = the difference between purchase price and face value.

Zero Coupon Bond Numerical
A zero coupon bond with face value ₹1,000 is sold for ₹750 today, maturing in 5 years.
Your return = ₹1,000 − ₹750 = ₹250 over 5 years (≈ 5.9% per year approximately). No interest is paid during the 5 years — only the ₹1,000 at the end.
What is a zero coupon bond?
A zero coupon bond pays no periodic interest (no coupons). Instead, it is issued at a price significantly below its face value and redeemed at face value on maturity. The investor's return comes entirely from this price appreciation. For example, if a ₹1,000 bond maturing in 10 years is sold for ₹558 today, the investor earns approximately 6% annually even without receiving any cash until maturity. T-Bills are a common example of zero coupon instruments. They appeal to investors who want predictable terminal value without reinvestment risk.

Coupon Rate, Yield, and Yield to Maturity (YTM)

Coupon Rate = (Annual Coupon Payment / Face Value) × 100
Current Yield = (Annual Coupon / Current Market Price) × 100

Yield to Maturity (YTM): The total return you earn if you hold the bond until maturity, accounting for coupon payments AND the difference between purchase price and face value.

Inverse relationship: When bond prices rise, yields fall. When bond prices fall, yields rise. Why? If a bond pays ₹80/year and you bought it for ₹1,000, yield = 8%. If the market price rises to ₹1,100, yield = 80/1100 = 7.27%. Same coupon, higher price = lower yield.

What is the difference between coupon rate and yield?
The coupon rate is fixed — it is the interest rate declared by the issuer, calculated on the face value of the bond. It never changes. Yield, however, changes with the market price of the bond. If a bond with a face value of ₹1,000 and a coupon rate of 8% (paying ₹80/year) is trading at ₹900 in the market, its current yield is 80/900 = 8.89%. Yield reflects the actual return you'll earn given today's market price, not the original face value. Yield to Maturity (YTM) goes further — it includes all coupon payments plus any capital gain/loss at maturity.

Banking Basics

RBI — The Reserve Bank of India

Established: April 1, 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934. Nationalised in 1949. Headquartered in Mumbai. Current Governor: Sanjay Malhotra (as of 2025).

Key functions of RBI: Issues currency notes (except ₹1 coin/note), acts as banker to the government, regulates commercial banks, controls monetary policy, manages foreign exchange reserves, manages public debt.

RBI is the banker's bank. Just as you keep your money in SBI, SBI keeps its reserve money with RBI. RBI sets the "rules of the game" for all banks in India.

Practical Example — RBI Rate Hike
In 2022-23, India's inflation crossed 7%. RBI raised repo rate from 4% to 6.5% (250 basis points). This made home loans, car loans, and business loans costlier. People borrowed less, spent less, reducing demand, which gradually pulled inflation down.
What is the repo rate? Why does RBI increase interest rates?
The repo rate is the rate at which the Reserve Bank of India lends money to commercial banks for short periods (usually overnight). RBI increases the repo rate as a tool to control inflation. When inflation rises — meaning prices are rising too fast — RBI hikes the repo rate. This makes borrowing costlier for banks, who then charge higher rates on loans to customers. Expensive loans mean people borrow and spend less. Lower demand for goods and services puts downward pressure on prices, controlling inflation. RBI's mandate, as of 2016, is flexible inflation targeting — keeping inflation at 4% (±2%).

Monetary Policy & Inflation Targeting

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): A 6-member committee (3 from RBI, 3 external members) that meets every 2 months to decide on interest rates. Chaired by the RBI Governor.

Flexible Inflation Targeting: Since 2016, RBI's mandate is to maintain CPI inflation at 4% (±2%) — meaning between 2% and 6%. If inflation breaches 6% for 3 consecutive quarters, RBI must explain to the government.

CPI vs WPI: CPI (Consumer Price Index) tracks prices of goods/services paid by urban consumers. WPI (Wholesale Price Index) tracks prices at the wholesale level. India uses CPI as the official inflation benchmark.

Section 08

Mutual Funds

What are Mutual Funds?

A mutual fund is a pool of money collected from many investors, managed by a professional fund manager, and invested in stocks, bonds, or other securities according to a defined objective.

Imagine 1,000 people each contribute ₹10,000 to a pool of ₹1 crore. A professional fund manager invests this pool across 50 companies. Each person owns a proportional share of this pool — that's a mutual fund.

NAV (Net Asset Value): The per-unit value of a mutual fund, calculated daily.
NAV = (Total Assets − Total Liabilities) / Number of Units Outstanding

When you invest, you buy units at NAV. When you redeem, you sell units at prevailing NAV. If NAV rises from ₹100 to ₹150, your investment grew 50%.

What is NAV? How is it different from a share price?
NAV (Net Asset Value) is the per-unit value of a mutual fund, calculated as total fund assets minus liabilities divided by the number of units outstanding. It is calculated once per day after market closing. A share price, by contrast, changes second by second based on real-time supply and demand. NAV doesn't indicate cheapness or expensiveness — a fund with NAV of ₹500 is not more expensive than one with NAV of ₹20. What matters is the return (% growth) since you invested, not the absolute NAV level.

SIP — Systematic Investment Plan

SIP is a method of investing a fixed amount at regular intervals (monthly, weekly) in a mutual fund, regardless of market conditions. It's not a product — it's a way of investing.

Benefits: (1) Rupee cost averaging — you buy more units when prices are low, fewer when high, reducing average cost. (2) Compounding — long-term reinvestment multiplies wealth. (3) Discipline — removes emotion from investing. (4) Small starting amounts (as low as ₹500/month).

SIP vs lump sum investment — which is better?
It depends on market conditions and individual circumstances. SIP is better for most retail investors because it eliminates the need to "time the market" — through rupee cost averaging, you automatically buy more units during market dips. It also instills financial discipline. Lump sum investment is better if you have a large amount available and the market is at a clear low (though this is difficult to predict). Research shows that for most investors over long periods, SIPs produce comparable returns to lump sum while significantly reducing stress and timing risk.

Section 09

Company Financials

The Three Financial Statements

Every listed company publishes three core financial statements every quarter:

1. Income Statement (P&L): Shows revenues and expenses over a period — tells you if the company made a profit.
2. Balance Sheet: Shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time — tells you what the company owns and owes.
3. Cash Flow Statement: Shows actual cash inflows and outflows — tells you if the company actually has the cash its profits suggest.

Revenue: Total money earned from selling goods/services. Top line.
EBITDA: Operational profitability before non-cash charges (depreciation) and financing (interest, tax). A popular measure of business health.

What is the difference between revenue and profit?
Revenue (or turnover) is the total amount a company earns from its core business activities — like total sales. It's called the "top line." Profit is what remains after subtracting all costs, expenses, interest, and taxes from revenue. It's the "bottom line." A company can have high revenue but low or even negative profit if costs are too high. For example, Amazon for years had massive revenue but minimal profit because it reinvested aggressively into growth. Revenue tells you the scale of business; profit tells you whether the business is financially sustainable.

Balance Sheet Basics

Assets: What the company owns — cash, inventory, buildings, machinery, investments.
Current Assets (convertible to cash within 1 year): cash, debtors, inventory
Non-current Assets (long-term): land, buildings, equipment, goodwill

Liabilities: What the company owes — loans, accounts payable, bonds.
Current Liabilities (due within 1 year): creditors, short-term loans
Non-current Liabilities: long-term debt, deferred taxes

Shareholders' Equity (Net Worth): What's left for owners = Assets − Liabilities

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity (The Fundamental Accounting Equation)

Important Financial Ratios

EPS — Earnings Per Share Profitability
EPS = Profit After Tax / Total Number of Outstanding Shares

Interpretation: Profit attributable to each share. Higher EPS = more profitable per share.

Example: Ravi's Apparel: PAT = ₹127.5 crore, Shares = 10 crore → EPS = ₹12.75 per share

What is EPS and why does it matter?
EPS (Earnings Per Share) is the net profit divided by the number of outstanding shares. It represents the profit attributable to each share and is a direct measure of a company's profitability per unit of ownership. Investors track EPS growth — rising EPS signals improving profitability. It is also used to calculate the P/E ratio, which is used for stock valuation. Diluted EPS also accounts for potential shares from stock options or convertible bonds, giving a more conservative picture.
P/E Ratio — Price to Earnings Valuation
P/E = Market Price per Share / EPS

Interpretation: How much investors pay for each rupee of earnings. A P/E of 20 means investors pay ₹20 for every ₹1 of current profit — betting on future growth.

Example: If Ravi's Apparel's share trades at ₹255 and EPS = ₹12.75 → P/E = 20x

High P/E: Market expects high future growth (growth stocks like Nifty IT companies typically have high P/E).
Low P/E: May indicate undervaluation or poor growth prospects.

A company has a P/E of 80. Is it expensive or cheap?
A P/E of 80 is high by historical standards, but whether it's expensive depends on context. High P/E companies are those with expectations of rapid future earnings growth — technology companies like Infosys or Zomato when it first listed often trade at high P/E because investors expect earnings to grow dramatically. If a company's earnings grow to justify the P/E, it's not expensive. However, if growth disappoints, a high-P/E stock can fall sharply. Always compare P/E to industry peers and the company's own historical P/E before concluding it's cheap or expensive.
P/B Ratio — Price to Book Valuation
P/B = Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share

Book Value = Total Assets − Total Liabilities (Net Worth) / Shares Outstanding

Interpretation: P/B of 1 means the stock trades at exactly its book value. P/B < 1 may indicate undervaluation (or serious problems). P/B > 1 means market values the company above its net assets — justified if the company generates strong returns.

ROE — Return on Equity Profitability
ROE = (Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity) × 100

Interpretation: How much profit the company generates for each rupee of shareholder money. ROE of 20% means for every ₹100 of equity, the company earns ₹20. Higher ROE = better use of shareholder capital. Sustainable ROE above 15% is considered excellent in India.

What is ROE and how does it differ from ROCE?
ROE (Return on Equity) measures profitability relative to shareholders' equity only — it shows how effectively the company uses shareholder money. ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) measures profitability relative to total capital employed (equity + long-term debt). ROCE is considered more comprehensive because it accounts for both equity and debt capital. A company can artificially inflate ROE by taking on more debt (financial leverage), so ROCE is a better indicator of operational efficiency. Both Reliance and HDFC Bank have consistently high ROCE, which is one reason they command premium valuations.
Debt-to-Equity Ratio Leverage
D/E Ratio = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity

Interpretation: How much debt the company has for every rupee of equity. D/E of 2 means for every ₹1 of owner money, there's ₹2 of borrowed money. Higher D/E = higher financial risk (more debt means more interest burden and risk of default). Different industries have different norms — capital-intensive industries (infrastructure, utilities) tolerate higher D/E than tech firms.

Current Ratio Liquidity
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Interpretation: Measures the ability to pay short-term debts with short-term assets. Ratio of 2:1 is generally considered healthy. Ratio below 1 means current liabilities exceed current assets — potential liquidity problem. Ratio too high (above 3–4) may indicate idle cash.

What ratios would you check before investing in a company?
Before investing, I would examine: (1) P/E ratio — to assess if the price is justified by earnings, compared to industry peers; (2) ROE/ROCE — to check if management uses capital efficiently; (3) Debt-to-Equity ratio — high debt increases financial risk, especially in downturns; (4) EPS growth trend — consistent EPS growth signals a healthy business; (5) Current ratio — to check short-term liquidity; and (6) Revenue and profit growth trends over 3–5 years. I'd also consider qualitative factors: quality of management, competitive moat, and industry tailwinds. No single ratio tells the whole story — they must be read together. Impact of Crude Oil on India

India imports over 85% of its crude oil needs. Crude oil is priced in USD. When oil prices rise:

  • India's import bill rises → trade deficit widens → rupee weakens
  • Fuel prices rise → transportation costs rise → inflation rises
  • Aviation, paint, chemicals, tyre industries face higher input costs → profit margins fall
  • Government may increase taxes or reduce subsidies to manage the burden

For every $10/barrel rise in crude, India's import bill rises by ~$14–15 billion/year. This is why Sensex often falls when global crude prices spike.

Impact of Interest Rates on Stock Markets

Interest rates and stock markets have an inverse relationship. When RBI raises rates:

  • Borrowing costs rise → companies invest less → slower earnings growth
  • Fixed deposits and bonds become more attractive → investors shift from stocks to bonds
  • Valuation multiples (P/E) compress — future earnings are "discounted" at higher rates
  • Stock prices fall, especially growth stocks with distant future earnings

Conversely, when RBI cuts rates (as it did in 2020 during COVID and in 2025), stocks tend to rise as money flows from low-yield deposits into equity markets.

100 Most Asked IPM Finance Questions

Compiled from IIM Indore, Rohtak, Ranchi, Shillong, Sirmaur, Kozhikode interviews and general IPM interview patterns. Questions are accompanied by model answers optimised for interview settings.

Q1What is Sensex? What was it today?
Sensex is the benchmark index of BSE comprising 30 large-cap stocks, calculated using free-float market capitalisation. Launched in 1986 with a base of 100. [Always check the current value before your interview — in 2025 it hovers around 78,000–85,000.] Its movement is used as a proxy for India's economic health and investor sentiment.
Q2What is Nifty? How is it different from Sensex?
Nifty 50 is the benchmark index of NSE, comprising 50 large-cap stocks. Both are free-float market cap weighted, but Nifty has 50 stocks (vs Sensex's 30), a broader sectoral representation, and started at a base of 1,000 in 1995. NSE handles more trading volume, making Nifty more relevant for derivative trading and institutional benchmarking.
Q3Why do stock prices move?
Stock prices move based on the law of supply and demand for shares. Fundamentally, prices move due to: (1) Changes in company earnings — better than expected results push prices up; (2) Macro factors — interest rate changes, inflation, GDP data; (3) Investor sentiment — fear and greed drive short-term movements; (4) News — management changes, new product launches, regulatory actions; (5) Global events — US Federal Reserve decisions, geopolitical tensions. Long-term, prices track earnings growth; short-term, they track sentiment and news.
Q4How are stock prices determined?
Stock prices are determined by supply and demand on the stock exchange — buyers bid and sellers ask, and the exchange matches them. At a fundamental level, a stock's "fair value" equals the present value of all future cash flows. If a company is expected to grow profits strongly, investors are willing to pay more today. Market price = what buyers and sellers agree on at any given moment, which may differ from intrinsic value — creating opportunities for value investors.
Q5What is market capitalisation?
Market capitalisation is the total market value of all outstanding shares = Share Price × Total Shares Outstanding. It measures company size. Reliance Industries, with ~₹19 lakh crore market cap, is India's most valuable company. SEBI classifies top 100 by market cap as large-cap, 101-250 as mid-cap, rest as small-cap.
Q6What is an IPO? Why do startups issue shares?
An IPO is when a private company first sells shares to the public. Startups issue shares primarily to raise capital for expansion without taking debt. Additionally, IPOs provide early investors and founders an exit opportunity. Shares also give companies a "currency" for acquiring other businesses. However, going public means regulatory compliance, quarterly disclosure requirements, and short-term performance pressure.
Q7What is SEBI? What does it do?
SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) is established under the SEBI Act, 1992, to regulate and develop securities markets in India. Its functions include protecting investor interests, regulating stock exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, and other intermediaries, approving IPO filings, preventing insider trading and market manipulation, and taking enforcement action against violators. SEBI is to markets what RBI is to banking.
Q8What is insider trading?
Insider trading is the buying or selling of a company's securities based on material, non-public information (MNPI). For example, if a Tata Motors executive knows the company is about to announce record profits before the public does and buys shares in advance, that's insider trading. It's illegal because it violates the principle of fair markets — not all investors have the same information. SEBI strictly prohibits this and has imposed significant penalties on violators. The Satyam scandal and several corporate insiders have been penalised in India.
Q9What is a bond?
A bond is a debt instrument where the investor (bondholder) lends money to an issuer (government or company) in exchange for periodic interest payments (coupons) and the return of the principal at maturity. Unlike a stock, a bondholder is a creditor, not an owner. In India, key bond types include G-Secs (government securities), T-Bills, and corporate bonds.
Q10What is a mutual fund?
A mutual fund pools money from multiple investors and invests it across securities (stocks, bonds, etc.) according to a defined objective, managed by a professional fund manager. Investors receive units proportional to their investment. Benefits include diversification, professional management, and accessibility with small amounts. The price of each unit is the NAV (Net Asset Value), calculated daily.
Q11What is a treasury bill?
A Treasury Bill (T-Bill) is a short-term government debt instrument issued by the Government of India through the RBI, maturing in 91, 182, or 364 days. T-Bills are zero coupon instruments — they don't pay interest. Instead, they are issued at a discount to face value. For example, a 91-day T-Bill with ₹100 face value may be issued at ₹98.40, and you receive ₹100 at maturity. They are the safest instruments in India since the government guarantees repayment.
Q12What is a zero coupon bond?
A zero coupon bond makes no periodic interest payments. It is issued at a deep discount to its face value and redeemed at face value on maturity. The difference between issue price and face value is the investor's total return. They are suitable for investors who want a known terminal payout without managing coupon reinvestment. T-Bills and certain infrastructure bonds follow this structure.
Q13What is a Demat account?
A Demat (dematerialised) account holds financial securities — stocks, bonds, ETFs — in electronic form, replacing physical share certificates. Maintained by depositories NSDL or CDSL through registered depository participants (banks, brokers). When you buy shares, they are credited to your demat account within T+1 days. To open one, you need KYC documents. A separate trading account is used to place buy/sell orders.
Q14What is inflation? Types of inflation?
Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, resulting in a fall in purchasing power of money. Key types: (1) Demand-pull inflation — too much money chasing too few goods (e.g., post-COVID spending surge); (2) Cost-push inflation — rising input costs push up prices (e.g., oil price rise causing fuel and transportation inflation); (3) Built-in (wage-price spiral) — workers demand higher wages → companies raise prices → workers demand higher wages again. India measures inflation via CPI (Consumer Price Index).
Q15What is the repo rate? What is the current repo rate?
The repo rate is the interest rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks for short periods. It is RBI's primary tool for controlling inflation and managing money supply. As of mid-2025, RBI's repo rate is 6.00% (after rate cuts in 2025). When RBI cuts repo rate, banks can borrow cheaper, pass it on as lower loan rates, stimulating economic activity. Always check the current rate before your interview.
Q16Why does RBI increase interest rates?
RBI increases interest rates (repo rate hike) primarily to control inflation. High rates increase borrowing costs for banks and ultimately for consumers and businesses. Higher loan rates discourage borrowing and spending, reducing demand in the economy. Lower demand means businesses can't raise prices as easily, bringing inflation down. This worked during 2022-23 when RBI raised rates from 4% to 6.5% to combat post-COVID inflation that touched 7%+.
Q17What is the difference between BSE and NSE?
BSE was founded in 1875 and is Asia's oldest exchange; NSE was founded in 1992. BSE has Sensex (30 stocks); NSE has Nifty 50 (50 stocks). NSE handles ~90% of equity trading volume in India due to its superior technology and dominance in F&O trading. BSE has more listed companies by number. For most practical purposes, listed companies trade on both exchanges with minimal price difference (arbitrage keeps them aligned).
Q18What happens if a company goes bankrupt?
Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016, a bankrupt company enters a resolution process. The order of payment to creditors is: (1) Resolution costs, (2) Secured creditors (banks with collateral), (3) Unsecured creditors, (4) Government dues, (5) Shareholders (equity holders). Shareholders are last in line — they often lose most or all of their investment. This is why equity investment is riskier than lending (bonds) to the same company.
Q19What are government securities (G-Secs)? Why are they important?
G-Secs are debt instruments issued by the Government of India (central or state) to borrow from the public to fund its fiscal deficit. They are issued in auctions conducted by the RBI. G-Secs are virtually risk-free (sovereign guarantee), liquid, and widely used by banks to meet SLR requirements. They also serve as benchmarks for pricing all other fixed income securities in India. The yield on 10-year G-Secs is India's benchmark "risk-free rate."
Q20What is the PE ratio? What does a high PE mean?
PE ratio (Price-to-Earnings) = Market Price per Share / EPS. A high PE means investors pay a premium for each rupee of current profit — usually because they expect high future growth. Nifty's historical PE averages around 18–22x. During COVID recovery, Nifty PE touched 40x. High PE doesn't automatically mean overvalued — Hindustan Unilever may deserve a high PE due to stable cash flows and brand strength. Always compare PE to the company's historical range and industry peers.
Q21What is the difference between investing and trading?
Investing is buying securities with a long-term horizon (years to decades), based on fundamental analysis of business quality and growth potential. Warren Buffett, who holds Coca-Cola shares for decades, exemplifies investing. Trading is buying/selling securities for short-term price movements (minutes to months), using technical analysis of price charts and patterns. Traders may execute dozens of trades daily; investors may not sell for years. Long-term, data consistently shows most traders underperform the index after costs and taxes, while patient investors outperform.
Q22What is EBITDA?
EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation. It measures operational profitability — how much cash the core business generates before accounting for capital structure (interest), tax, and non-cash charges (depreciation). It is widely used for comparing companies across different capital structures and tax rates. However, EBITDA can be misleading if a company has high debt (interest is a real cost) or heavy capex needs (depreciation reflects real asset wear). "EBITDA is not cash flow" is a common finance saying.
Q23What is the CRR?
CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio) is the percentage of a bank's total deposits that it must mandatorily keep with the RBI as cash reserve, earning no interest. If CRR is 4% and a bank has deposits of ₹100 crore, it must keep ₹4 crore with RBI. By raising CRR, RBI reduces money banks can lend — tightening money supply and cooling inflation. By lowering CRR, RBI enables more lending, stimulating the economy. CRR is a powerful monetary policy tool.
Q24What is the SLR?
SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) is the percentage of deposits that commercial banks must maintain as liquid assets — specifically in cash, gold, or government approved securities. Currently at around 18%. SLR ensures banks always have enough liquid assets to meet sudden withdrawals and also creates a captive market for government securities. Unlike CRR, SLR-maintained assets earn returns (G-Secs pay interest).
Q25What is an index fund? Is it better than an active fund?
An index fund passively mirrors a market index (like Nifty 50) by holding the same stocks in the same proportions. It requires no stock-picking research and charges very low expense ratios (0.1–0.3% vs 1.5–2% for active funds). Research globally shows that over 10–15 years, 80–90% of active equity funds underperform their benchmark index after expenses. This makes index funds compelling for long-term investors. However, active funds can outperform in inefficient markets or niche sectors. For most individual investors, index funds are the rational choice.
Q26What is the difference between a stock and a bond?
A stock represents ownership in a company — stockholders are part-owners who benefit from growth (capital appreciation + dividends) but bear all risks, including losing their entire investment. A bond represents debt — the bondholder is a creditor who receives fixed interest regardless of company performance and gets principal back at maturity. Stocks offer higher potential returns but higher risk; bonds offer lower, predictable returns. In bankruptcy, bondholders are paid before stockholders. Most investment portfolios hold both for balance.
Q27What is a dividend?
A dividend is a distribution of a company's profits to its shareholders, typically as cash per share. It represents shareholders' share of profits. Example: "Infosys declares ₹22 interim dividend per share." Dividends are paid from PAT. Companies are not obligated to pay dividends — many growth companies (like Amazon historically, or most Indian startups) reinvest all profits. Dividend yield = Annual Dividend per Share / Current Market Price × 100. PSU companies (ONGC, Coal India) are known for high dividend yields in India.
Q28What is ROE?
Return on Equity = Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity × 100. It measures how efficiently a company generates profits from shareholders' capital. An ROE of 20% means for every ₹100 of equity, the company earns ₹20. Companies like Asian Paints, HDFC Bank, and Nestle India consistently deliver ROE above 20%, which is one reason their stocks command premium valuations. Low ROE suggests either low profitability or inefficient capital deployment.
Q29What is cash flow? How is it different from profit?
Profit is an accounting figure that can include non-cash items (depreciation, accruals). Cash flow measures actual cash that has moved in and out of the company. A company can show accounting profit while having negative cash flow (e.g., if all sales are on credit and debtors haven't paid). Conversely, a profitable company can go bankrupt if it runs out of cash. The saying "revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality" captures this — cash flow is the ultimate test of financial health.
Q30What is the difference between a merger and a joint venture?
A merger is when two separate companies combine to form one entity — one or both companies may cease to exist as separate entities (e.g., HDFC Ltd merging into HDFC Bank in 2023). A joint venture is when two or more companies create a new, separate entity together for a specific purpose, while both parent companies continue to exist independently (e.g., Maruti Suzuki was a JV between Maruti (GOI) and Suzuki Japan, or SBI-SBI Life). Key difference: in a merger, one/both companies dissolve; in a JV, both parents retain their independent existence. [Note: This question appeared in real IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q31What is the difference between revenue recognition and cash receipt?
Revenue recognition follows the accrual principle — revenue is recorded when it is earned (when goods are delivered or services rendered), not when cash is received. A company may sell goods worth ₹1 crore in March on 30-day credit terms — the ₹1 crore is recognised as revenue in March even though the cash arrives in April. This is why a company can show high revenue but low cash. Cash receipt refers to when actual cash arrives. The difference creates accounts receivable (debtors) on the balance sheet.
Q32What are economies of scale?
Economies of scale refer to the cost advantages that arise as production volume increases. As a company produces more units, fixed costs (like factory rent, machinery) are spread over more units, reducing cost per unit. For example, a car manufacturer making 10 cars spreads factory costs over 10 cars; making 10,000 cars spreads the same fixed cost over 10,000 — drastically lower cost per car. This is why large companies like Amazon can offer lower prices than small retailers — their scale allows lower per-unit costs. [Note: This appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.] Beyond a point, diseconomies of scale set in — management becomes unwieldy, coordination costs rise.
Q33What is the Gini coefficient?
The Gini coefficient (or Gini index) measures income or wealth inequality within a population. It ranges from 0 to 1: 0 means perfect equality (everyone earns the same), and 1 means perfect inequality (one person earns everything). India's Gini coefficient hovers around 0.35–0.38 based on income, but is much higher (above 0.6) when measured by wealth. Countries like Nordic nations have low Gini (around 0.25), while Latin American nations often exceed 0.45. Reducing the Gini coefficient is a key development goal. [This appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q34What is a balance sheet? What does it tell you?
A balance sheet is a financial statement showing what a company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the residual value belonging to owners (shareholders' equity) at a specific point in time. The fundamental equation is: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity. It tells you: (1) Whether the company has more assets than liabilities (financial solvency), (2) How much debt it carries vs equity, (3) What the company's net worth is, (4) Whether current assets can cover current liabilities (liquidity). Unlike the income statement which covers a period, the balance sheet is a snapshot on a specific date.
Q35What is an FD? How does it differ from a mutual fund?
A Fixed Deposit (FD) is a banking product where you deposit money for a fixed period at a guaranteed interest rate. It is risk-free (up to ₹5 lakh insured by DICGC), returns are fixed, and maturity value is predetermined. A mutual fund invests pooled money in market securities — returns are market-linked (not guaranteed), can be higher or lower than FD rates, and the investment value fluctuates. FDs are for capital preservation with assured returns; mutual funds are for wealth creation with market-linked returns. Historically, equity mutual funds have returned 12–15% over 10+ years vs FD rates of 6–7%.
Q36What is breakeven?
Breakeven is the point where total revenue equals total costs — neither profit nor loss. Formula: Breakeven point (in units) = Fixed Costs / (Selling Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit). This is the minimum sales needed to cover all costs. For example, if fixed costs are ₹10 lakh, selling price is ₹500/unit, and variable cost is ₹300/unit: Breakeven = 10,00,000 / (500−300) = 5,000 units. Below 5,000 units = loss; above = profit. Breakeven analysis is crucial for new businesses to assess viability. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q37What is goodwill in accounting?
Goodwill is an intangible asset that arises when one company acquires another for more than the fair market value of its net tangible assets. The excess paid represents the value of brand reputation, customer relationships, and management quality that aren't physically tangible. For example, if Tata acquires a company with net assets worth ₹100 crore but pays ₹150 crore, the ₹50 crore excess is goodwill. Goodwill appears on the balance sheet under intangible assets. If the acquired company underperforms, the goodwill may need to be "impaired" (written down), which reduces profits. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q38What is the law of demand?
The law of demand states that, all else equal, as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and vice versa — price and demand have an inverse relationship. This is because higher prices reduce affordability and purchasing power. The demand curve slopes downward from left to right. Exceptions include Giffen goods (inferior goods where price rises actually increase demand, as consumers can't afford alternatives) and Veblen goods (luxury goods where high price increases desirability).
Q39What are Giffen goods?
Giffen goods are a special category of inferior goods that violate the law of demand — when their price rises, demand actually increases. This paradox occurs because for very poor consumers, the good is a staple that consumes most of their income. When the price rises, they can no longer afford superior substitutes and must consume even more of the cheaper Giffen good. Classic example: coarse rice or bread for very poor populations. Named after Scottish economist Robert Giffen. [This question appeared in multiple IIM Shillong and Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q40What is elasticity of demand?
Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. Formula: PED = % Change in Quantity Demanded / % Change in Price. If PED > 1: elastic (demand very sensitive to price changes — luxury goods, non-necessities). If PED < 1: inelastic (demand not sensitive — petrol, medicines, basic food). If PED = 1: unit elastic. Luxury goods have elastic demand; necessities are inelastic. This determines pricing strategy: companies with inelastic demand can raise prices more freely. [Appeared frequently in IIM Shillong transcripts.]

Questions 41–100 — Quick Reference Format

Q41What is quantitative easing?
Quantitative easing (QE) is a monetary policy where a central bank creates new money to buy government bonds and other financial assets from banks, injecting liquidity into the economy when interest rates are already near zero. Used by the US Federal Reserve during 2008 and 2020 COVID crisis. It lowers long-term interest rates and boosts asset prices. India has not implemented traditional QE but uses similar tools like open market operations.
Q42What is the difference between economic profit and accounting profit?
Accounting profit = Revenue − Explicit (paid) Costs. Economic profit = Revenue − Explicit Costs − Implicit (opportunity) Costs. If a business owner pays himself no salary, accounting profit may be ₹5 lakh, but economic profit subtracts the ₹4 lakh he could have earned as a salaried employee. Normal profit is the breakeven point in economic terms — when economic profit = 0. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcript.]
Q43What is consumer surplus?
Consumer surplus is the difference between what a consumer is willing to pay for a good and what they actually pay. If you are willing to pay ₹500 for a shirt but buy it for ₹350, your consumer surplus = ₹150. On a supply-demand diagram, it is the area below the demand curve and above the market price. It measures buyer welfare — higher consumer surplus means buyers are getting more value. [Appeared in multiple IIM transcripts.]
Q44What is producer surplus?
Producer surplus is the difference between what a producer actually receives for a good and the minimum price they would have accepted. If a farmer would have sold rice at ₹15/kg but receives ₹20/kg, the ₹5 is producer surplus. On a diagram, it is the area above the supply curve and below the market price. Together, consumer surplus + producer surplus = total social welfare from a market transaction. [Appeared in IIM Shillong and Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q45What is the difference between perfect competition and monopoly?
In perfect competition: many sellers, identical products, no single seller can influence price, free entry/exit, price = marginal cost. In a monopoly: single seller with no close substitutes, sets price above marginal cost, barriers to entry protect the monopolist. Perfect competition examples: agricultural markets. Monopoly examples: government utility companies, Indian Railways. In between lies oligopoly (few large sellers — telecom: Jio, Airtel, Vi) and monopolistic competition (many sellers, differentiated products — restaurants, FMCG brands). [This appeared in IIM transcripts.]
Q46What is GDP? Which method does India use?
GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year. Three measurement methods: (1) Production/Value-added method — sum of value added at each stage; (2) Income method — sum of all incomes earned; (3) Expenditure method — sum of all spending (C+I+G+NX). India primarily uses the production/value-added approach. India's GDP (nominal) is approximately $3.7 trillion (2024), making it the 5th largest economy. GDP per capita is about $2,600. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q47What is purchasing power parity (PPP)?
PPP adjusts for cost of living differences between countries to compare economies more fairly. The Big Mac Index by The Economist is a fun example — a Big Mac costs $5 in the US but equivalent to $2 in India; this suggests the rupee is undervalued by market exchange rates. By PPP, India is actually the 3rd largest economy (after US and China), even though by nominal GDP it's 5th. PPP-adjusted comparisons better reflect actual standards of living. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcript.]
Q48What is depreciation? What are the two main methods?
Depreciation is the allocation of the cost of a tangible asset over its useful life, reflecting wear and tear. Two main methods: (1) Straight Line Method (SLM) — equal depreciation each year. If a machine costs ₹10 lakh and lasts 10 years with zero salvage value, depreciation = ₹1 lakh/year. (2) Written Down Value (WDV) / Declining Balance method — depreciation is a fixed percentage of the book value each year, resulting in higher depreciation in early years, lower later. WDV better reflects actual asset use for many assets. [Both methods appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q49What is black money and how does it affect national income?
Black money refers to income or wealth on which taxes have not been paid — typically earned through illegal activities or undeclared transactions. It distorts national income calculations because unreported transactions are not captured in GDP. This leads to underestimation of actual economic size. Black money also creates inflation (as underground spending raises demand), diverts resources from productive uses, worsens income inequality, and reduces government tax revenue. Demonetisation in November 2016 targeted black money held as cash. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q50What is the Hindenburg report and short selling?
Short selling involves borrowing shares, selling them at a high price, waiting for the price to fall, buying them back cheaper, and returning them — profiting from the decline. A short seller publishes a research report (as Hindenburg Research did with Adani Group in January 2023) to publicly highlight concerns, which may trigger a sell-off. The report alleged accounting fraud and stock manipulation in Adani Group companies. This caused the Adani Group's market cap to fall by nearly $150 billion in days. Short selling provides price discovery but is controversial when used aggressively. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q51What is inflation's impact on the rich and poor?
Inflation disproportionately hurts the poor: (1) The poor spend a larger share of income on necessities (food, fuel) which are the most inflation-prone. (2) The rich hold financial assets (stocks, real estate) that tend to appreciate with inflation. (3) Debtors benefit (inflation reduces real debt burden) while creditors/savers lose. (4) Fixed-income workers (daily wage labourers) don't see wage increases as fast as price increases. This is why high inflation typically widens the rich-poor gap and is a serious social justice issue. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q52What is the principle of accounting revenue recognition?
The revenue recognition principle states that revenue should be recognised (recorded) when it is earned and realised or realisable — not necessarily when cash is received. Under Ind AS 115 (aligned with IFRS 15), revenue is recognised when: a performance obligation is satisfied (i.e., goods delivered or service rendered). This is part of the accrual concept of accounting. Example: A construction company recognises revenue over the course of a project as milestones are completed, not only at the end. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcript.]
Q53What are accounting standards? What does India follow?
Accounting standards are rules that govern how financial transactions must be recorded and reported, ensuring consistency and comparability. India follows Ind AS (Indian Accounting Standards), which are converged with IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) — adopted for listed companies and large private companies. Smaller companies use older AS (Accounting Standards) under ICAI. Indian-listed subsidiaries of global companies may need to report under both Ind AS and IFRS. The ICAI (Institute of Chartered Accountants of India) sets accounting standards in India. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q54What is the Minimum Support Price (MSP)?
MSP is the price at which the government agrees to buy agricultural produce from farmers if market prices fall below a certain level. It provides a safety net for farmers. The government (through agencies like FCI for wheat and rice) procures crops at MSP. MSP is announced before sowing, so farmers know their minimum guaranteed income. Critics say MSP distorts market signals and primarily benefits large farmers. Supporters argue it protects vulnerable farmers from price crashes. MSP for major crops is recommended by the CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices). [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q55What is digital marketing? How has technology changed consumer behaviour?
Digital marketing is promoting products/services through digital channels — social media, search engines, email, apps. Key channels: SEO, SEM/Google Ads, social media (Instagram, YouTube), influencer marketing, email marketing. Technology has transformed consumer behaviour by enabling: (1) Price comparison in seconds, (2) Peer reviews and user-generated content influencing decisions more than ads, (3) Personalisation — algorithms showing relevant products, (4) Instant purchasing, (5) Subscription economy. The consumer now has more power and information than ever before. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur and Kozhikode transcripts.]
Q56What are the four Ps of marketing?
The 4Ps marketing mix, introduced by E. Jerome McCarthy: (1) Product — what you're selling (features, quality, branding); (2) Price — how much you charge (cost-based, value-based, competitive pricing); (3) Place — how you distribute it (retail, online, direct sales); (4) Promotion — how you communicate it (advertising, PR, sales promotion). Modern extensions include 7Ps (adding People, Process, Physical Evidence) for services. Every business decision revolves around optimising this mix. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q57What is the difference between a customer and a consumer?
A customer is the person who purchases a product. A consumer is the person who uses/consumes it. They may or may not be the same person. Example: A mother (customer) buys Maggi noodles (product) which her child (consumer) eats. Understanding this distinction matters for marketing — advertising should target the decision-maker (customer) while the product experience must satisfy the end-user (consumer). In B2B markets, the purchasing department (customer) and the end-users (consumers) are different. [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]
Q58What is the difference between revenue and profit? (common trap question)
Revenue is the total money earned from all sales — it's the "top line." Profit is revenue minus all costs, expenses, interest, and taxes — it's the "bottom line." A company can have high revenue but no profit (e.g., a startup spending heavily on growth). Amazon had $280 billion in revenue for years while barely breaking even. Conversely, a small boutique consultancy can have modest revenue with very high profit margins. Interviewers use this to test whether candidates confuse business size (revenue) with business health (profitability).
Q59What is a startup? When does a startup become a unicorn?
A startup is a new business venture working to solve a problem with a scalable, often technology-driven model. The key distinction from traditional businesses is the pursuit of rapid growth and scalability. A unicorn is a privately held startup with a valuation of $1 billion or more. The term was coined by venture capitalist Aileen Lee in 2013 to denote rarity. India has 100+ unicorns, including Byju's, Swiggy, Razorpay, Dream11, and CRED. When a unicorn goes public (IPO), it becomes a "decacorn" at $10B+, etc.
Q60What is venture capital?
Venture capital (VC) is a form of private equity where investors provide funding to early-stage startups with high growth potential in exchange for an equity stake. VCs accept high risk because the potential returns (if the startup succeeds) are enormous. Famous Indian VC-backed successes: Flipkart, Zomato, Paytm. The VC model: invest in 10-20 startups, expect most to fail, but one or two will generate returns that cover all losses and deliver massive profits. Sequoia Capital, Accel, and SoftBank are major VC investors in India.
Q61What is privatisation? Pros and cons?
Privatisation is the transfer of ownership of a government enterprise to private hands. India has privatised Air India (to Tata Group, 2022), Hindustan Zinc, and parts of VSNL. Pros: operational efficiency, innovation, reduced fiscal burden on government, better customer service. Cons: natural monopolies (power, water) may exploit consumers if not regulated, job losses, public interest may be sacrificed for profit, strategic sectors (defence, telecom) may face security risks under private ownership. The optimal approach depends on the sector and quality of regulation. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]
Q62What is a current account deficit?
The current account in India's balance of payments records trade in goods (imports-exports), services, remittances, and investment income. A current account deficit (CAD) means India imports more than it exports in goods/services — India pays more to the world than it earns. India typically runs a CAD because it imports large amounts of crude oil, gold, and electronics. In FY2024, India's CAD was about 0.7% of GDP. A high CAD weakens the rupee (more foreign exchange goes out) and increases dependence on foreign capital inflows.
Q63What is a fiscal deficit?
Fiscal deficit is the difference between the government's total expenditure and its total revenue (excluding borrowings). If the government spends ₹45 lakh crore and earns ₹38 lakh crore in taxes/non-tax revenue, the deficit = ₹7 lakh crore, which it must finance through borrowing. India's fiscal deficit target for FY2025 is 4.9% of GDP, on a path to 4.5%. High fiscal deficit increases public debt, crowds out private investment (as government borrowing absorbs capital), and can cause inflation if financed by printing money. The FRBM Act (2003) sets deficit targets.
Q64What is the RBI Governor's name?
As of 2025, the RBI Governor is Sanjay Malhotra, who took charge in December 2024 succeeding Shaktikanta Das. Always check the current name before your interview — it's a basic factual question that catches many candidates off-guard. Also remember: RBI was established on April 1, 1935; the first Indian Governor was C.D. Deshmukh (1943); RBI headquarters is in Mumbai.
Q65What is the Nifty value today? How do you interpret it?
Always check the Nifty 50 value on the morning of your interview. As of 2025, Nifty trades around 24,000–26,000. Interpret it by: (1) Comparing to previous close — is the market up or down and why? (2) Comparing to historical levels — is it near its 52-week high or low? (3) Noting the P/E ratio of the index (historically around 18-22x; high P/E suggests expensive market). Mentioning a specific recent event (budget announcement, RBI policy, global cue) that moved the market today will impress interviewers. Check Moneycontrol or NSE website.
Q66What is a Non-Performing Asset (NPA)?
An NPA (Non-Performing Asset) is a bank loan where the borrower has not made any interest or principal payments for 90 days. NPA represents bad loans on a bank's balance sheet. High NPAs reduce bank profitability, restrict lending capacity, and can threaten financial stability. India faced a serious NPA crisis between 2015-2019, particularly in infrastructure and steel sectors. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) was a key reform to resolve NPAs. Gross NPA ratio for Indian banks has improved from ~11% (2018) to around 3–4% by 2024.
Q67What is a budget? What is the Union Budget?
A budget is an estimate of revenues and expenditures over a future period. The Union Budget is the Government of India's annual financial statement, presented by the Finance Minister in Parliament (typically on February 1). It outlines the government's revenue plan (taxes) and spending plan (welfare, defence, infrastructure, subsidies). It also reveals the fiscal deficit target. The Union Budget is one of the most watched events in India — markets, industries, and businesses align plans around it. Budget statements can move stock markets significantly.
Q68What is the difference between direct and indirect tax?
Direct taxes are paid directly by the person on whom they are levied and cannot be shifted — income tax, corporate tax, capital gains tax. Indirect taxes are collected by an intermediary (business) and ultimately borne by the end consumer through higher prices — GST, customs duty, excise duty. GST (Goods and Services Tax), implemented in July 2017, unified India's complex indirect tax system into one. The Finance Ministry and Income Tax Department manage direct taxes (CBDT); the CBIC manages indirect taxes.
Q69What is GST?
GST (Goods and Services Tax) is a comprehensive, destination-based, multi-stage tax on goods and services, implemented in India on July 1, 2017. It replaced multiple cascading taxes (service tax, VAT, excise duty, etc.). GST has four slabs: 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%, with essential goods at 0%. It follows the CGST (Central GST) + SGST (State GST) model for intra-state transactions, and IGST for inter-state. The GST Council (chaired by Finance Minister) decides rates. GST simplified tax compliance and reduced the "tax on tax" (cascading effect) problem.
Q70What is SWIFT?
SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) is a global messaging network used by financial institutions to securely transmit information and instructions for international money transfers. When India's banks send or receive money from abroad, they use SWIFT codes. In 2022, Russia was partly disconnected from SWIFT as a sanction for invading Ukraine — a significant financial weapon. India's SBI has SWIFT code "SBININBB." SWIFT doesn't move money directly; it sends the messages instructing correspondent banks to move money.

Q91: What is a sinking fund? — A fund set aside by a company or government over time to repay a future debt obligation. Like an EMI savings plan for a large loan maturity. [Appeared in IIM Shillong transcripts.]

If IPO price is ₹100 for a ₹10 face value share, ₹90 goes into the share premium account (Securities Premium Reserve) on the balance sheet.

Q97: What is an indifference curve? — A curve showing all combinations of two goods that give a consumer the same level of utility (satisfaction). The consumer is "indifferent" between any two points on the curve. Properties: downward sloping (more of one means less of the other), convex to origin (diminishing marginal rate of substitution), non-intersecting. The highest attainable indifference curve on a budget constraint is consumer equilibrium. [Appeared extensively in IIM transcripts.]

Q98: What is opportunity cost? — Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone when a decision is made. If you spend ₹1 lakh on starting a food truck business instead of investing in Nifty (which would have earned 12%), your opportunity cost is the 12% return. All economic choices involve opportunity costs. Economists argue that no decision is truly "free" — even using free time has opportunity cost (what else could you have done?). [Appeared in IIM Sirmaur transcripts.]

Q99: What is the difference between wealth and income? — Income is a flow — money earned over a period (salary, profits, rent). Wealth is a stock — accumulated assets minus liabilities at a point in time. High-income people may have low wealth if they spend everything; modest-income people can build high wealth through saving and investing over decades. Oxfam reports often highlight wealth inequality rather than income inequality because wealth disparities are even more extreme.

Q100: What is a repo rate cut's impact on home loan EMIs? — When RBI cuts repo rate, banks' cost of borrowing from RBI falls. Banks typically pass this on by reducing their MCLR (Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate) or External Benchmark Rate. Home loans linked to the repo rate (most new home loans post-2019) automatically get cheaper — EMIs fall or tenure shortens. A 50 bps (0.5%) repo rate cut on a ₹50 lakh, 20-year home loan reduces EMI by approximately ₹1,600/month. This stimulates housing demand and construction activity.
Section 13

Advanced Topics for Strong Candidates

Yield Curve & Inverted Yield Curve

A yield curve plots the yields (interest rates) of bonds with equal credit quality but different maturity dates. Normally, longer-term bonds have higher yields — you demand more compensation for locking money longer.

Normal yield curve: Short-term rates < long-term rates. Signals healthy economy with growth expectations.
Inverted yield curve: Short-term rates > long-term rates. Historically, one of the most reliable predictors of a recession. When the US 2-year treasury yield exceeds the 10-year yield, recession typically follows in 12-18 months. This happened in 2022-23 in the US, predicting the 2023 slowdown.

Normally, you'd demand more interest on a 10-year FD than a 1-year FD. If banks suddenly offer more for 1-year FDs, they expect big trouble ahead — they'll rather have your money for 1 year than be locked into lower rates for 10 years. An inverted yield curve is that signal at a national scale.

Quantitative Easing (QE)

When interest rates are already near zero and the economy still needs stimulus, central banks use QE — creating new money to buy bonds and other assets from banks. This floods banks with cash, lowering long-term interest rates and pushing investors into riskier assets (stocks, real estate).

The US Fed's QE programs post-2008 and post-2020 contributed to massive stock market rallies. The risk: too much QE can cause inflation and asset bubbles. When the Fed "tapered" QE in 2013 (the "Taper Tantrum"), global markets including India fell sharply.

Derivatives: Futures and Options

Futures: A contract to buy/sell an asset at a predetermined price on a future date. Both buyer and seller are obligated. Used by farmers to lock in crop prices, airlines to lock in fuel prices, and speculators.

Options: Gives the buyer the right but not the obligation to buy (Call option) or sell (Put option) an asset at a specified price (strike price) before expiry. The buyer pays a premium for this right.

Options Example
You buy a Call option on Reliance at a strike price of ₹2,800 for a premium of ₹50. If Reliance goes to ₹3,000, you exercise your option and profit ₹200 − ₹50 premium = ₹150. If Reliance stays at ₹2,600, you let the option expire, losing only the ₹50 premium. Maximum loss for option buyer = premium paid.

Hedging

Hedging is reducing risk by taking an offsetting position. Like an insurance policy — you pay a small cost (premium) to protect against a large loss.

Real Hedging Example
An airline fuel hedging: IndiGo buys crude oil futures at $70/barrel. If oil rises to $90, IndiGo's higher fuel bill is offset by profits on its futures position. If oil falls to $50, IndiGo pays more on its futures loss but benefits from lower fuel costs. Either way, uncertainty is reduced.

Credit Default Swaps (CDS)

A CDS is a financial contract where the protection buyer pays periodic premiums to the protection seller. In return, if a specified credit event occurs (like a bond default), the seller compensates the buyer for the loss.

It's essentially insurance on a bond. During the 2008 financial crisis, the collapse of the CDS market (where trillions of dollars of protection had been written without sufficient capital) was a key trigger for the global financial crisis.

Sovereign Bonds

Sovereign bonds are debt instruments issued by national governments. When India issues rupee-denominated bonds to Indian investors, they are domestic sovereign bonds (G-Secs). When governments issue bonds in foreign currencies (USD, EUR) to international investors, they are external sovereign bonds (like Masala Bonds — rupee-denominated bonds issued internationally by India).

A government's sovereign bond yield is inversely related to its creditworthiness. India's 10-year G-Sec yield (around 7% in 2024) is used as the risk-free benchmark for all Indian investments.

What is economies of scale? What's the reason behind it?

Model Answer: Economies of scale are cost advantages from producing at larger volumes. As output rises, average cost per unit falls. Reasons: (1) Spreading fixed costs over more units — a factory costing ₹100 crore produces 1,000 cars at ₹10 lakh/unit vs 10,000 cars at ₹1 lakh/unit fixed cost component; (2) Operational efficiencies — specialisation, better machinery utilisation; (3) Bulk purchasing — suppliers offer discounts for large orders; (4) Financial — large companies get cheaper credit; (5) Marketing — advertising cost per unit falls with scale. Beyond a point, diseconomies of scale emerge — communication costs, management complexity, bureaucracy.

📍 IIM Shillong (Multiple Commerce Panels)
What are indifference curves? What is their real-life application?

Model Answer: Indifference curves represent all combinations of two goods that give a consumer the same total utility — they are "indifferent" between all points on the curve. Properties: downward sloping (MRS — Marginal Rate of Substitution), convex to origin (diminishing MRS), non-intersecting, higher curves = higher utility. Real-life application: Portfolio allocation — an investor is indifferent between combinations of risk and return along an indifference curve; consumer equilibrium where a budget line is tangent to an indifference curve maximises utility. Marketing: understanding consumer tradeoffs between price and quality.

Why asked: This was asked in almost every commerce stream interview at IIM Shillong. It's a core microeconomics concept.
📍 IIM Shillong (24 July Commerce)
What ratios would you check before investing in a company?

Model Answer: Before investing: (1) P/E ratio — valuation vs earnings, compared to industry peers; (2) Debt/Equity ratio — financial risk assessment; (3) ROE/ROCE — management efficiency; (4) EPS growth trend (3-5 years) — business momentum; (5) Current ratio — short-term liquidity; (6) Revenue and profit CAGR — growth trajectory; (7) Dividend yield — income component; (8) Promoter holding — high promoter stake signals confidence. I'd also read the annual report's risk section and assess qualitative factors like management integrity and competitive positioning.

📍 IIM Kozhikode (Bangalore, Multiple)
Why do interest rates differ for housing loans and car loans?

Model Answer: Interest rates differ based on: (1) Collateral quality — a home loan is secured by an immovable property (house) which appreciates over time, creating strong collateral. A car depreciates rapidly — within 5 years a car is worth 40-50% of its original value. Lower collateral quality = higher rate. (2) Loan tenure — home loans are 15-20 years vs 3-7 for cars. Longer tenure = more interest rate risk. (3) Default risk — historically, home loan defaults are lower because people protect their home above other obligations. (4) Policy — government often subsidises home loans (PMAY) to encourage housing. Typical rates: home loans ~8.5-9.5%, car loans ~9.5-11.5%.

Why asked: This appeared in multiple Kozhikode transcripts — tests whether the candidate can apply basic finance reasoning to everyday life without needing formal finance education.
📍 IIM Sirmaur (Multiple Panels)
What is a stock exchange? What are BSE and NSE? What is stock analysis?

Model Answer: A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace facilitating buying and selling of listed company shares. BSE (1875, Asia's oldest) lists 5,000+ companies; NSE (1992, electronic trading pioneer) handles ~90% of equity volume. Stock analysis determines a stock's value or price direction using two approaches: (1) Fundamental analysis — evaluating company's financials, business model, competitive position, management quality; uses ratios like P/E, P/B, ROE. (2) Technical analysis — studying historical price charts and patterns to predict future price movements; uses indicators like moving averages, RSI, Bollinger Bands. Long-term investors typically use fundamental analysis; traders use technical analysis.

📍 IIM Sirmaur (15-16 July)
What is a balance sheet? What are current and non-current assets? What is liquidity?

Model Answer: A balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Current assets are convertible to cash within one year — cash, trade receivables, inventory, short-term investments. Non-current assets are long-term — property, plant and equipment, goodwill, intangibles. Liquidity is the ease and speed with which an asset can be converted to cash without significant value loss. Cash is perfectly liquid; real estate is illiquid. Liquidity ratios (Current Ratio, Quick Ratio) measure a company's ability to meet short-term obligations.

📍 IIM Sirmaur (21 July)
What is cost accounting and cost sheet?

Model Answer: Cost accounting is the process of recording, classifying, and analysing all costs associated with producing a product or service, used for internal decision-making. A cost sheet is a document that shows all costs incurred to produce a certain number of units over a period, classified into: Prime Cost (direct materials + direct labour), Factory Cost (prime cost + manufacturing overheads), Cost of Production (factory cost + opening WIP − closing WIP), Cost of Goods Sold, and finally Total Cost including selling and distribution expenses. It helps determine selling price, monitor cost efficiency, and identify cost reduction opportunities.

📍 IIM Sirmaur (28 July)
What is RBI? When was it formed? What are its functions?

Model Answer: RBI (Reserve Bank of India) was established on April 1, 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, and nationalised in 1949. Functions: (1) Currency issuance — sole authority to issue banknotes (except ₹1 coin/note); (2) Banker to government — manages government accounts, public debt; (3) Banker to banks — manages CRR/SLR, provides refinancing; (4) Monetary policy — sets repo rate, manages inflation; (5) Foreign exchange management — manages forex reserves, administers FEMA; (6) Financial supervision — regulates commercial banks, NBFCs, payment systems; (7) Development role — promotes financial inclusion, credit flow to priority sectors.

📍 IIM Kozhikode (July, Multiple)
Why does the government change the interest rate? What is the role of RBI?

Clarification + Model Answer: It is RBI (not the government) that changes interest rates through the Monetary Policy Committee. The government influences through fiscal policy (taxes, spending). RBI changes the repo rate to: (1) Control inflation — hikes to cool rising prices; (2) Stimulate growth — cuts to make credit cheaper when economy slows; (3) Manage currency — rate decisions affect the rupee's value vs USD; (4) Financial stability — ensuring banks remain solvent and credit flows normally. This is why maintaining RBI's independence from government pressure is critical for credible monetary policy.

⚠ Common Mistake: Saying "the government changes interest rates." It's RBI (through the MPC). Interviewers at IIMK were specifically testing this — one candidate got the answer right by carefully attributing rate-setting authority to RBI, not Finance Ministry.
📍 IIM Shillong (25 July Bangalore)
What is elasticity of dog food? Is it elastic or inelastic?

Model Answer (as given by candidate in transcript): Dog food demand is elastic because: (1) Multiple competing brands exist (Pedigree, Royal Canin, Whiskas, local brands) — consumers easily switch; (2) Price is a significant factor in purchase decisions for most pet food buyers; (3) Substitutes exist (home-cooked food for pets). However, for premium pet food segments (specialty medical food prescribed by vets), demand is inelastic as pet owners prioritise their pet's health over cost. The overall category is elastic at the mass market level.

Why asked: Tests ability to apply economic concepts to unexpected, real-life scenarios — not just textbook examples. This is a favourite IIM technique.
📍 IIM Shillong (Commerce, 24 July)
What is accounting for a company taking a loan? What is share capital — where is it shown? Paid-up value or face value?

Model Answer: Share capital is shown on the Liabilities side of the balance sheet, under "Equity and Liabilities" heading, specifically under "Shareholders' Equity." Share capital is recorded at face value (par value), not market value. Authorised capital is the maximum a company can issue; Issued capital is what it has issued; Subscribed capital is what shareholders have agreed to take; Paid-up capital is what has actually been received from shareholders. Premium over face value is shown separately as Securities Premium Reserve.

50 Interview Traps & How to Avoid Them

Trap 01 — Sensex is on NSE
Many candidates confuse the two. Sensex = BSE. Nifty = NSE.
✓ Fix: Always pair them — "Sensex is BSE's index, Nifty 50 is NSE's index."
Trap 02 — "The government sets interest rates"
It's RBI (through MPC) that sets monetary policy rates, not the government/Finance Ministry.
✓ Fix: "RBI's Monetary Policy Committee, chaired by the Governor, sets the repo rate."
Trap 03 — "High NAV means expensive mutual fund"
NAV of ₹500 is not more expensive than NAV of ₹20. What matters is % growth, not absolute NAV.
✓ Fix: Compare NAV growth % since inception, not absolute NAV level.
Trap 04 — Confusing primary and secondary market
"I bought Zomato shares in the IPO from the secondary market." IPO = primary market.
✓ Fix: Primary = company issues new shares. Secondary = investor-to-investor trading on exchange.
Trap 05 — Saying SEBI was established in 1992
SEBI was established in 1988 as a non-statutory body; given statutory powers by the SEBI Act in 1992.
✓ Fix: "SEBI was established in 1988 and given statutory authority in 1992."
Trap 06 — Saying RBI was established in 1949
RBI was established in 1935 and nationalised in 1949.
✓ Fix: "RBI was founded April 1, 1935. It was nationalised in 1949."
Trap 07 — Confusing coupon rate and yield
"The coupon rate changes with the market." No — coupon rate is fixed. Yield changes.
✓ Fix: Coupon rate = fixed, based on face value. Yield = changes with market price.
Trap 08 — Saying Giffen goods are luxury goods
Giffen goods are inferior staple goods (rice, bread for poor). Luxury goods where price signals status are called Veblen goods.
✓ Fix: Giffen = inferior, essential goods. Veblen = luxury status goods. Different concepts.
Trap 09 — Saying "profit" when asked about revenue
"Reliance's profit last year was ₹10 lakh crore." No — that's revenue. Profit is far lower.
✓ Fix: Always distinguish. Revenue = total sales. Profit = what's left after costs.
Trap 10 — Saying RBI issues ₹1 notes
The ₹1 coin and note are issued by the Ministry of Finance/Government, not RBI.
✓ Fix: All notes except ₹1 are issued by RBI. ₹1 is issued by the Government of India.
Trap 11 — Confusing FII and FDI
FII/FPI invests in stocks/bonds (portfolio investment, easily reversible). FDI is direct investment in businesses/factories (long-term, harder to reverse).
✓ Fix: FII = portfolio investment in securities. FDI = stake in actual businesses or setting up operations.
Trap 12 — Saying "bear market means stocks go up"
Bull = market rises (bull charges UP). Bear = market falls (bear swipes DOWN).
✓ Fix: Bull ↑ (economy is "charging forward"). Bear ↓ (economy is "sliding down").
Trap 13 — Overstating your investment knowledge
If asked "do you invest in stocks?" and you say "yes, I track F&O derivatives daily," be ready for intense grilling.
✓ Fix: Say what you actually know. "I follow markets through newspapers and apps, I haven't invested yet but plan to." Honesty builds credibility.
Trap 14 — Saying "the current account is about current issues"
Current account in balance of payments tracks trade in goods, services, remittances — NOT "current events."
✓ Fix: Current account = trade in goods + services + remittances + investment income. Capital account = investments and loans.
Trap 15 — Saying "every company should have a high PE ratio"
Interviewers test whether you understand PE contextually. High PE stocks can be overvalued traps.
✓ Fix: High PE is justified for high-growth companies. For mature slow-growth companies, high PE means overvaluation. Always compare to industry average and historical range.
Trap 16 — "Index funds are for beginners only"
Warren Buffett has repeatedly said index funds are better than most active funds for most investors, including sophisticated ones.
✓ Fix: Index funds are not beginner products — they are a sophisticated, evidence-based approach. Acknowledge this in your answer.
Trap 17 — Confusing CRR and SLR
CRR must be kept as cash with RBI, earns no interest. SLR is kept as liquid assets (gold, G-Secs), which earn interest.
✓ Fix: CRR = pure cash with RBI (sterile). SLR = liquid but earning assets.
Trap 18 — "Mutual funds guarantee returns"
Mutual funds investing in equity or debt carry market risk. No mutual fund (except specific guaranteed-return products) promises a fixed return.
✓ Fix: Market-linked mutual funds carry risk. Only specific capital protection funds offer limited guarantees.
Trap 19 — Giving today's exact Sensex/Nifty from days ago
Interviewers sometimes ask "what is the Nifty today?" and the interview may be on a different day than you last checked.
✓ Fix: Always check Nifty/Sensex on the morning of your interview. Say "as of this morning, Nifty is approximately X, up/down Y% from yesterday."
Trap 20 — Saying "shareholders get paid before bondholders in bankruptcy"
In liquidation, creditors (including bondholders) are paid before equity shareholders. Shareholders are last.
✓ Fix: Priority in bankruptcy: secured creditors → unsecured creditors → preference shareholders → equity shareholders.
Trap 21 — Confusing accounting profit and economic profit
Accounting profit ignores opportunity costs (implicit costs). Economic profit includes both explicit and implicit costs.
✓ Fix: Economic profit = Accounting profit − Opportunity cost (implicit costs). Normal profit = when economic profit = 0.
Trap 22 — Saying "a demat account is the same as a trading account"
Different accounts, different roles. Both needed to invest in stocks.
✓ Fix: Demat = holds securities electronically. Trading = platform to place buy/sell orders. Trading account is linked to demat account and bank account.
Trap 23 — Not knowing India's current economic figures
Being asked GDP growth, inflation, repo rate and not knowing reveals you don't follow current affairs.
✓ Fix: Before every interview, check: Nifty/Sensex level, repo rate, CPI inflation, quarterly GDP growth, rupee vs dollar rate.
Trap 24 — Saying "Nifty represents all Indian stocks"
Nifty 50 represents only 50 stocks — though these represent about 65-70% of total NSE market cap.
✓ Fix: "Nifty 50 consists of 50 of India's largest and most liquid companies, representing about 65-70% of NSE's total market capitalisation."
Trap 25 — "Zero coupon bonds pay zero returns"
Zero coupon bonds have no periodic COUPON (interest), but generate return through price appreciation (bought at discount, redeemed at face value).
✓ Fix: "Zero coupon" means no coupon payments — but the total return is the difference between discounted issue price and face value at maturity. Often quite attractive.
Trap 26 — Over-claiming knowledge of a company you mentioned
If you say "I'm interested in Adani Green Energy" or "I admire Tata Motors," be prepared for deep questions on their business, financial metrics, current news.
✓ Fix: Only mention companies you've genuinely researched. Know their latest quarterly result, key metrics, recent news.
Trap 27 — Confusing BSE's Sensex number with NSE's Nifty number
They are different scales. Sensex ~80,000; Nifty ~24,000. Never quote Sensex's number for Nifty or vice versa.
✓ Fix: Note they are different indices on different scales. Check each separately.
Trap 28 — "Banks create money out of thin air"
Partially correct but misleading — banks create "credit money" through the money multiplier, but this is constrained by CRR, SLR, and capital adequacy requirements.
✓ Fix: "Banks create credit through the fractional reserve banking system — for every ₹100 deposited and with CRR of 4%, banks can lend ₹96, which when re-deposited allows further lending, multiplying the money supply."
Trap 29 — Confusing fiscal deficit and trade deficit
Fiscal deficit = government spending > revenue. Trade deficit = imports > exports. Both can coexist but are different.
✓ Fix: Fiscal = government budget. Trade = goods/services exports vs imports. Current account includes trade + services + remittances.
Trap 30 — "Lower share price means cheap stock"
Price ₹10 stock is not cheaper than ₹1,000 stock. Cheapness is measured by PE ratio, PB ratio, relative to intrinsic value — not absolute price.
✓ Fix: Valuation is relative. A ₹10 stock with PE of 100 is far more "expensive" than a ₹1,000 stock with PE of 10.
Trap 31-50: Quick Fire Traps
31: Don't say "inflation is always bad" — mild inflation (2-4%) is good for economic growth.
32: Don't say "the stock market = the economy" — markets price the future; economy measures the present.
33: Don't confuse merger (combine) and acquisition (one buys the other).
34: Don't say "FPO and IPO are the same" — FPO is subsequent issue; IPO is the first.
35: Don't confuse "interest rate" and "inflation rate" — related but different.
36: Don't say "SEBI regulates RBI" — both are independent regulators. RBI for banking; SEBI for capital markets.
37: Don't confuse inelastic with "no demand" — inelastic means demand doesn't change much with price, not that demand disappears.
38: Don't confuse hedging with speculation — hedging reduces risk; speculation accepts risk for reward.
39: Don't say "derivatives are always risky" — derivatives are used for risk reduction (hedging) by companies and investors.
40: Don't say "bonds are risk-free" — government bonds are near risk-free; corporate bonds have credit risk; all bonds have market risk (price changes with interest rates).
41: Don't confuse consumer surplus and producer surplus — consumer's gain from lower price; producer's gain from higher price.
42: Don't say "GDP only measures trade" — GDP measures all economic production within a country's border.
43: Don't say "equity = money invested" — equity = total assets minus total liabilities = owner's stake.
44: Don't confuse CPI and WPI — CPI = consumer-level prices (India's inflation benchmark); WPI = wholesale-level.
45: Don't say "NPA means loan is unpaid" — technically, NPA = 90+ days without any payment. Regular delayed payment is "Special Mention Account (SMA)" first.
46: Don't confuse "rights issue" and "bonus shares" — rights issue = offer to buy more shares (at discount); bonus = free shares from reserves.
47: Don't confuse "face value" and "book value" — face value = nominal/par value on share certificate; book value = net assets per share from balance sheet.
48: Don't say "a company's profit = cash in bank" — profit can include non-cash revenues; cash flow statement shows actual cash position.
49: Don't confuse "repo" and "reverse repo" — repo = RBI lends to banks; reverse repo = RBI borrows from banks.
50: Never say "I don't follow markets" in a finance interview — always say you follow key indices, read business news, and track macro indicators. Even 15 minutes daily on Moneycontrol or ET is sufficient.
