Measures of Central Tendency

Mean · Median · Mode — the three pillars every IIM panel asks about

Mean (Arithmetic Average) Core +

The mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. It considers every data point and is the most widely used measure of central tendency.

Arithmetic Mean
x̄ = (Σxᵢ) / n

Weighted Mean = Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / Σwᵢ

When to use: When data is roughly symmetric and there are no extreme outliers. e.g., average test scores, average revenue.

Limitation: Highly sensitive to outliers. If Mukesh Ambani walks into a room of 100 people, the mean income of the room skyrockets — but no one in the room actually "has" the mean income.

💼 Business Applications
  • Average Order Value (AOV) in e-commerce
  • Mean salary in HR compensation benchmarking
  • Average revenue per user (ARPU) for SaaS companies
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF) in quality control
  • Portfolio average return in finance
Median Core +

The median is the middle value when data is arranged in order. For an even number of observations, it is the average of the two middle values.

Median Position
Odd n → [(n+1)/2]th term
Even n → Average of (n/2)th and [(n/2)+1]th terms

Grouped data: M = L + [(n/2 − cf) / f] × h
where L=lower class boundary, cf=cumulative freq, f=freq of median class, h=class width

Key insight: The median is resistant to outliers. This is why median household income is a better measure of a typical household's earning than mean income.

💼 Business Applications
  • Median salary in job listings (fairer representation than mean)
  • Median house price — unaffected by a few luxury properties
  • IIM placement reports often use median salary to represent typical outcomes
  • Median delivery time in logistics
💡 Interview Tip: "Why do IIM placement brochures show median salary and not mean?" — The median is not distorted by a few students who got unusually high packages.
Mode Core +

The mode is the value that appears most frequently in a dataset. A dataset can have no mode (all values unique), one mode (unimodal), two modes (bimodal), or more.

Mode for Grouped Data (Czuber's Formula)
Mode = L + [f₁ − f₀] / [2f₁ − f₀ − f₂] × h

where f₁ = highest frequency, f₀ = preceding frequency, f₂ = following frequency

When to use: Categorical data, or when you want the "most popular" option.

💼 Business Applications
  • Most popular product size in inventory management (e.g., size M in clothing)
  • Most common purchase time in retail analytics
  • Most frequently cited customer complaint in feedback analysis
  • Modal payment method on a platform (UPI, card, COD)
Empirical Relationship (Karl Pearson)
Mode ≈ 3 × Median − 2 × Mean
📌 This empirical relation holds for moderately skewed distributions. It lets you estimate one if you know the other two.

🗂 Interview Questions — Central Tendency

  • 1
    What is the difference between mean, median, and mode? Why do we need all three?
    IIM Amritsar · Ranchi
    ✅ Model Answer
    Mean is the arithmetic average — sum divided by count. Sensitive to outliers. Median is the middle value when sorted — robust to extreme values. Mode is the most frequent value — useful for categorical data.

    We need all three because they capture different aspects of the data's centre. In a perfectly symmetric distribution, all three are equal. In a right-skewed distribution (like income), mean > median > mode, meaning mean overstates the "typical" value. The three together give a complete picture.
  • 2
    Give a business example where median is more useful than mean.
    IIM Ranchi · Amritsar
    ✅ Model Answer
    Executive compensation and employee salaries: If a company has 100 employees earning ₹30,000/month and a CEO earning ₹50,00,000/month, the mean salary would be approximately ₹5.27 lakh — far above what 99% of employees actually earn. The median of ~₹30,000 is far more representative of the typical employee's experience. This is why placement reports and government wage surveys use median figures.
  • 3
    If mean=50 and median=45, which way is the data skewed?
    IIM Amritsar
    ✅ Model Answer
    When mean > median, the data is right-skewed (positively skewed). There are some very high values pulling the mean upward while the median stays near the centre. The tail extends towards the right. Example: income distribution — most people earn moderate incomes, but a few billionaires pull the mean to the right.
  • 4
    You manage a garment store. How would you use mean, median, and mode for business benefit?
    IIM Amritsar (actual question)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Mode: Order the most units of the most sold size (e.g., size M is the mode of sizes sold) — critical for inventory management.
    Median: Find the median purchase value to set discount thresholds — half your customers spend above/below this amount.
    Mean: Calculate average revenue per customer to forecast total revenue and set budgets.
    Combined: If mean sale value >> median sale value, a few high-ticket items are pulling the average up — you should stock more premium items and also ensure mid-range availability for typical customers.
  • 5
    What does it mean when mean = median = mode?
    Conceptual
    ✅ Model Answer
    When all three measures of central tendency are equal, the distribution is perfectly symmetrical (bell-shaped or normal distribution). The data has no skew. This is the ideal condition assumed by many statistical tests. In real life, it rarely holds exactly but is approximated by things like human heights within a homogeneous population or IQ scores across a large sample.

Types of Means: AM, GM, HM

Three means for three different contexts — and their crucial relationship

Arithmetic Mean (AM)

Sum of values divided by count. Most common. Best for additive data (temperatures, scores).

AM = (a + b) / 2
for two values

Geometric Mean (GM)

nth root of the product of n values. Best for multiplicative/proportional data (growth rates, ratios).

GM = ⁿ√(x₁·x₂·...·xₙ)
= antilog(Σlog x / n)

Harmonic Mean (HM)

Reciprocal of arithmetic mean of reciprocals. Best for rates (speed, price-earnings ratio).

HM = n / Σ(1/xᵢ)
For two: HM = 2ab/(a+b)
Golden Inequality (for positive values)
HM ≤ GM ≤ AM

Equality holds only when all values are equal.

Also: GM² = AM × HM (for two positive numbers)
📌 Real Insight: If you drive from Delhi to Agra at 60 km/h and return at 40 km/h, your average speed is NOT (60+40)/2 = 50 km/h. It's the HM = 2(60×40)/(60+40) = 48 km/h. The AM is wrong here because you spend more time at the slower speed.
💼 Business Applications
  • GM: CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) uses geometric mean — ₹100 growing to ₹200 in 3 years: CAGR = (200/100)^(1/3) − 1 = 26%
  • GM: Average return on investment across multiple periods (because returns compound)
  • HM: Average P/E ratio when you have equal rupee investment across stocks
  • HM: Fleet average fuel efficiency when equal distance is covered by each vehicle
  • AM: Average sales across regions, average cost per unit

🗂 Interview Questions — AM, GM, HM

  • 6
    What is the relationship between AM, GM, and HM?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    For any set of positive numbers: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM, with equality holding when all values are equal.
    For two positive numbers a and b:
    • AM = (a+b)/2
    • GM = √(ab)
    • HM = 2ab/(a+b)
    • GM² = AM × HM (a very useful identity)

    This relationship is known as the AM-GM-HM inequality and is fundamental in optimization problems.
  • 7
    Why do we use geometric mean to calculate average investment returns?
    Finance Application
    ✅ Model Answer
    Investment returns compound — each period's return builds on the previous. Suppose an investment grows +50% one year and falls −50% the next. AM = 0%, suggesting no loss. But actually: ₹100 → ₹150 → ₹75. The GM = √(1.5 × 0.5) − 1 = √0.75 − 1 ≈ −13.4%, correctly capturing the actual loss. Geometric mean is the only correct measure when values are multiplicative or when dealing with percentage changes over time.
  • 8
    A person travels equal distances at speeds of 30, 40, and 50 km/h. What is the average speed?
    Conceptual (HM)
    ✅ Model Answer
    When equal distances are covered at different speeds, use HM:
    HM = 3 / (1/30 + 1/40 + 1/50)
    = 3 / (0.0333 + 0.025 + 0.02)
    = 3 / 0.0783 ≈ 38.3 km/h

    Note: If equal TIME was spent at each speed, you'd use AM = (30+40+50)/3 = 40 km/h. The denominator (distance vs time) determines which mean to use.

Measures of Dispersion

Range · Variance · Standard Deviation · CV — how spread out is the data?

Variance and Standard Deviation Most Asked +

Variance (σ²) measures the average squared deviation from the mean. Squaring ensures all deviations are positive and penalises large deviations more.

Standard Deviation (σ) is the square root of variance — bringing it back to the original unit. It is the most important measure of spread.

Population Variance
σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N

σ = √[Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N] ← Standard Deviation

Sample Variance (Bessel's correction)
s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1) ← divides by n−1 for unbiased estimate

Why square the deviations? If we just averaged deviations, positive and negative ones would cancel out (the sum of deviations from the mean is always zero). Squaring solves this.

Why SD and not variance? Variance is in squared units (e.g., ₹²). SD is in the original unit (₹), making it interpretable alongside the mean.

📌 68-95-99.7 Rule (Normal Distribution): In a normal distribution — 68% of data lies within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ.
💼 Business Applications
  • Risk in finance: SD of stock returns = volatility. Higher SD = riskier investment
  • Quality control: Six Sigma aims to reduce process SD so that defects are 6σ away from mean
  • HR: SD of employee performance scores to identify outlier performers
  • Marketing: SD of customer spending to segment audiences
  • Supply chain: SD of demand variability to set safety stock levels
Coefficient of Variation (CV) Relative Measure +

CV is SD expressed as a percentage of the mean. It allows comparison of variability across datasets with different units or scales — an absolute SD of ₹10 means very different things if the mean is ₹50 vs ₹5,000.

Coefficient of Variation
CV = (σ / μ) × 100%

Lower CV = More consistent/stable
Higher CV = More variable/risky
💼 Business Applications
  • Comparing volatility of two stocks (e.g., stock A: mean=₹500, SD=₹50 → CV=10%; Stock B: mean=₹100, SD=₹15 → CV=15%. Stock B is MORE volatile despite lower absolute SD)
  • Comparing consistency of two manufacturing processes
  • Investor choosing between two mutual funds with different NAVs
Range and Mean Deviation +

Range

Range = Maximum − Minimum
Simplest measure of spread. Very sensitive to outliers. Useful for quick estimates (e.g., stock price range, temperature range).

Mean Deviation

MD = Σ|xᵢ − A| / n, where A is mean/median.
Uses absolute values instead of squares — less popular than SD but intuitive.

🗂 Interview Questions — Dispersion

  • 9
    What is standard deviation and why is it preferred over variance?
    IIM Amritsar · Ranchi · Shillong (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Standard deviation is the square root of variance — the average squared deviation from the mean. SD is preferred because: (1) It is in the same unit as the original data, making it interpretable alongside the mean. (2) Variance is in squared units (e.g., rupees²), which has no real-world meaning. (3) SD can be directly used to state confidence intervals: "average order value is ₹500 ± ₹80" is meaningful; "variance is ₹6,400 rupees²" is not.
  • 10
    If mean=10 and SD=2, what does this tell you about the data?
    IIM Ranchi (actual question)
    ✅ Model Answer
    The data clusters around 10, with most values within 2 units of it. If it's normally distributed:
    • ~68% of values fall between 8 and 12 (mean ± 1SD)
    • ~95% fall between 6 and 14 (mean ± 2SD)
    • ~99.7% fall between 4 and 16 (mean ± 3SD)
    CV = 2/10 × 100% = 20% — the data is moderately variable. This level of spread is used in quality control: if it were manufacturing tolerances, ±2σ means 5% of products would be outside spec.
  • 11
    Why do we square deviations when calculating variance? Why not just average the absolute deviations?
    Conceptual
    ✅ Model Answer
    Three reasons: (1) Deviations always sum to zero — positive and negative deviations from the mean cancel exactly, so averaging them gives 0 regardless of spread. (2) Squaring penalises large deviations more — a deviation of 4 contributes 16 to variance, while one of 2 contributes only 4. This makes variance more sensitive to outliers, which is often desirable. (3) Mathematical tractability — squaring makes variance differentiable and compatible with optimization and calculus-based techniques. Mean absolute deviation also exists and is used in some fields (like forecasting MAE), but variance and SD dominate statistics because of their mathematical properties.
  • 12
    Explain standard deviation to a layman without using maths.
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    "Imagine you and your 4 friends take a test. You all score 50 — the average is 50 and standard deviation is 0, meaning everyone performed identically. Now imagine scores of 10, 30, 50, 70, 90 — average is still 50, but standard deviation is high, meaning scores are all over the place. SD tells you how predictable the group is. A low SD means 'everyone performs similarly'; a high SD means 'results vary wildly.' In business, when a factory has high SD in product dimensions, quality is inconsistent — some products are too big, some too small."
  • 13
    What is the difference between standard deviation and variance?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Variance = SD². While variance measures average squared deviation, SD is the square root, restoring the original unit. If we're measuring heights in cm, variance would be in cm² (meaningless physically), while SD is in cm (directly comparable to individual heights). Formula: Variance σ² = Σ(xᵢ−μ)²/N; SD σ = √(σ²). Practically, both measure spread — SD is reported; variance is used in calculations (additivity property: variances of independent variables add up, SDs don't).

Skewness & Kurtosis

Shape of a distribution — which way does the tail go?

Skewness — Asymmetry

TypeRelationshipTailExample
SymmetricMean=Median=ModeNo tailIQ scores, height
Right Skew (+)Mean > Median > ModeRightIncome, wealth
Left Skew (−)Mean < Median < ModeLeftAge at retirement
Karl Pearson's Coefficient
Sk = (Mean − Mode) / SD
or Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / SD

|Sk| < 0.5 → Low skew (nearly normal)

Kurtosis — Peakedness

Mesokurtic (Kurtosis = 3)

Normal bell curve. The benchmark. Excess kurtosis = 0.

Leptokurtic (K > 3)

Taller, sharper peak; fatter tails. More extreme values than normal. Stock market returns often exhibit this ("fat tails").

Platykurtic (K < 3)

Flatter peak; thinner tails. Values more uniformly spread. Example: rolling a die.

🗂 Interview Questions — Skewness

  • 14
    What is skewness? Draw the graph of a skewed distribution and show where mean, median and mode lie.
    IIM Ranchi (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution around its mean. In a right-skewed (positive) distribution: the tail extends to the right; Mode < Median < Mean — the mean is pulled rightward by high outliers. Example: income distribution.

    In a left-skewed (negative) distribution: tail extends left; Mean < Median < Mode. Example: exam scores where most students score high but a few score very low.

Probability Foundations

The language of uncertainty — essential for every IIM interview

Core Probability Concepts Must Know +
Basic Probability
P(A) = Favourable outcomes / Total outcomes
0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1; P(impossible) = 0; P(certain) = 1

P(A') = 1 − P(A) [Complement Rule]
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) [Addition Rule]
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) [Multiplication — IF independent]

Mutually Exclusive Events

Cannot occur simultaneously. P(A ∩ B) = 0
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

Example: Getting Heads AND Tails on one flip.

Independent Events

Occurrence of one does not affect the other. P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

Example: Two consecutive coin flips. Result of first doesn't affect second.

Exhaustive Events

Together they cover all possible outcomes.
P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ... ∪ Aₙ) = 1

Example: {H, T} for a coin flip.

Equally Likely Events

Each outcome has the same probability.
Example: Fair die — each face has P = 1/6.

🗂 Classic Probability Questions (from actual IIM interviews)

  • 15
    Two dice are thrown. What is the probability that the sum is 9?
    IIM Shillong · Sirmaur (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Total outcomes = 6 × 6 = 36
    Favourable (sum = 9): (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) = 4 outcomes
    P(sum = 9) = 4/36 = 1/9 ≈ 0.111
  • 16
    Two dice are thrown. What is the probability of getting a sum of 7?
    IIM Ranchi (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Favourable outcomes (sum=7): (1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1) = 6 outcomes
    P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6
    Note: 7 is the most probable sum when rolling two dice — this is why 7 is special in casino games like Craps!
  • 17
    What is the probability of drawing a face card from a standard deck of 52 cards?
    IIM Amritsar (actual, multiple panels)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Face cards = Jack, Queen, King (3 per suit × 4 suits = 12 face cards)
    P(face card) = 12/52 = 3/13 ≈ 0.231
    Follow-up: P(red king) = 2/52 = 1/26 (2 red kings in deck)
  • 18
    What is the probability of getting 53 Sundays in a non-leap year?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    A non-leap year has 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 extra day.
    The extra day can be any one of: Mon, Tue, Wed, Thu, Fri, Sat, Sun.
    P(53 Sundays) = 1/7

    For a leap year (366 days = 52 weeks + 2 extra days):
    The 2 extra days can be: (Mon,Tue), (Tue,Wed), (Wed,Thu), (Thu,Fri), (Fri,Sat), (Sat,Sun), (Sun,Mon) — 7 possibilities, 2 contain Sunday.
    P(53 Sundays in leap year) = 2/7
  • 19
    What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads when 5 fair coins are tossed?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Using Binomial distribution: P(X=3) = C(5,3) × (0.5)³ × (0.5)²
    = 10 × 0.125 × 0.25 = 10 × 0.03125 = 5/16 = 0.3125

    Alternative (count method): Total = 2⁵ = 32. Favourable = C(5,3) = 10.
    P = 10/32 = 5/16 ✓
  • 20
    What is the probability of NOT winning if P(winning) = 2/5?
    Conceptual
    ✅ Model Answer
    P(not winning) = 1 − P(winning) = 1 − 2/5 = 3/5
    This uses the Complement Rule: P(A') = 1 − P(A). It's one of the most useful shortcuts in probability — often easier to calculate what you DON'T want and subtract from 1. For example, "probability of at least one success" = 1 − P(no successes).

Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Updating beliefs with new information — used in AI, medical testing, and business

Conditional Probability
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) [probability of A given B has occurred]

Bayes' Theorem
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)

P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) + P(B|A')·P(A') [Total Probability]

Intuition
Prior belief × Likelihood = Updated belief (posterior)
💼 Business Applications
  • Medical testing: P(disease | positive test) — a positive test does not mean 100% disease probability. Bayes updates this using disease prevalence
  • Spam filtering: Gmail calculates P(spam | contains "free money") using Bayes
  • Credit risk: P(default | late payment history)
  • Marketing attribution: P(customer converted | saw our ad)
  • Machine learning: Naïve Bayes classifier used for text classification

🗂 Interview Questions — Conditional Probability & Bayes

  • 21
    What is conditional probability? How is it different from regular probability?
    IIM Amritsar · Kozhikode (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Regular probability P(A) is calculated in the full sample space. Conditional probability P(A|B) is the probability of A given that B has already occurred — we restrict our sample space to B.

    Example: P(rain today) = 0.3. But P(rain today | there are dark clouds) = 0.7. The knowledge of dark clouds updates our probability. Formula: P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B).

    Business example: P(customer churns) = 20%. But P(customer churns | hasn't logged in for 30 days) = 65%. This conditional probability drives targeted retention campaigns.
  • 22
    Explain Bayes' theorem with a real-world example.
    IIM Amritsar · Indore (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Bayes' theorem updates probability when new evidence arrives.

    Medical example: A disease affects 1% of the population. A test is 95% accurate (if you have the disease, 95% chance it's positive; if you don't, 2% false positive rate).

    If you test positive, what's P(you have the disease)?
    P(D) = 0.01, P(+|D) = 0.95, P(+|no D) = 0.02
    P(+) = 0.95×0.01 + 0.02×0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0198 = 0.0293
    P(D|+) = (0.95 × 0.01) / 0.0293 ≈ 32.4%

    Only a 32% chance, even with a positive test! This counterintuitive result is why mass screening for rare diseases can be problematic.
  • 23
    What are mutually exclusive vs independent events?
    IIM Amritsar · Ranchi (actual, multiple times)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Mutually Exclusive: Events that cannot both occur. P(A∩B) = 0. If one occurs, the other definitely doesn't. Example: Getting a 3 or a 5 on a single die roll — you can get one or the other but not both simultaneously.

    Independent: Occurrence of one has no effect on probability of the other. P(A|B) = P(A), and P(A∩B) = P(A)×P(B). Example: Flipping two separate coins — first flip result doesn't affect second.

    Key insight: Mutually exclusive events (when both P(A)>0 and P(B)>0) are NOT independent — if A occurs, B definitely doesn't, so A definitely affects B's probability!

Probability Distributions

The mathematical models of randomness in the real world

Bernoulli Distribution — The Simplest Trial Foundation +

A single trial with exactly two outcomes: success (probability p) or failure (probability 1−p). It is the building block of the Binomial distribution.

Bernoulli Distribution
P(X = 1) = p (success)
P(X = 0) = 1−p = q (failure)

Mean (E[X]) = p
Variance = p(1−p) = pq
SD = √(pq)
💼 Business Applications
  • Will a customer click on an ad? (Yes/No)
  • Will a loan default? (Default/No Default)
  • Will a customer buy after visiting the landing page? (Conversion rate p)
  • Quality check: Is a product defective? (Pass/Fail)
Binomial Distribution — n Independent Trials Most Asked +

Number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, each with success probability p.

Conditions (BINS): Binary outcomes, Independent trials, fixed Number of trials, Same probability each trial.

Binomial PMF
P(X = k) = C(n,k) × pᵏ × (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

Mean = np
Variance = np(1−p)
SD = √(np(1−p))

Notation: X ~ B(n, p)
📌 As n→∞ and p→0 with np = λ constant, Binomial → Poisson. As n→∞, Binomial → Normal (CLT).
💼 Business Applications
  • If 5% of products are defective, what's the probability of 2 defective items in a batch of 20?
  • Email marketing: if open rate is 20%, probability that 8 out of 30 recipients open the email
  • A/B testing: comparing conversion rates between two website versions
  • Insurance: probability that exactly k out of n policyholders file a claim
Poisson Distribution — Rare Events Over Time/Space Key Distribution +

Models the number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, when events occur independently at a constant average rate λ (lambda).

Poisson PMF
P(X = k) = (e⁻λ × λᵏ) / k!

Mean = λ
Variance = λ
(Mean = Variance — unique property!)

e ≈ 2.71828 (Euler's number)

Key conditions: Events are rare, occur independently, average rate λ is constant over time/space, two events cannot happen at the exact same instant.

💼 Business Applications
  • Number of customer service calls per hour
  • Number of website visits per minute (server capacity planning)
  • Number of accidents per month on a highway
  • Number of defects per square metre of fabric in textile manufacturing
  • Number of emails received per hour in an inbox
  • Bank: number of customers arriving per hour (queuing theory)
💡 Poisson vs Binomial: Use Poisson when n is very large, p is very small, and np = λ is moderate. Rule of thumb: n > 20, p < 0.05.
Normal Distribution — The Bell Curve Most Important +

The most important distribution in statistics. Symmetric, bell-shaped, defined entirely by mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ).

Normal PDF
f(x) = [1 / (σ√(2π))] × e^[−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)]

Notation: X ~ N(μ, σ²)

Standard Normal (Z)
Z = (X − μ) / σ ← Standardisation formula
Z ~ N(0, 1) [Mean=0, SD=1]

68-95-99.7 Rule
P(μ−σ < X < μ+σ) = 68.27%
P(μ−2σ < X < μ+2σ) = 95.45%
P(μ−3σ < X < μ+3σ) = 99.73%

Properties: (1) Symmetric about mean (2) Mean = Median = Mode (3) Total area under curve = 1 (4) Asymptotic — never touches x-axis (5) Defined for all real x

💼 Business Applications
  • Quality control (Six Sigma): Defects should be 6σ from mean — implies 3.4 defects per million
  • Finance: Stock returns approximately normal (though fat tails exist) — used in Black-Scholes options pricing
  • HR: Employee performance ratings, IQ scores, heights
  • Marketing: Customer lifetime value distribution
  • Risk management: Value at Risk (VaR) calculations assume normality
⚠️ IIM Question: "What is the area under the standard normal distribution from 0 to infinity?" Answer: 0.5 (since the total area = 1 and the distribution is symmetric about 0).

    What is a Poisson distribution and where is it used?
    IIM Ranchi · Sirmaur (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Poisson distribution models the number of events occurring in a fixed time period when events happen at a constant average rate λ and independently of each other.

    Key property: Mean = Variance = λ

    Real examples: customer calls to a call centre per hour (λ=20), website visits per minute, accidents per month.

Correlation & Regression

Measuring and modelling relationships between variables

Correlation — Measuring Linear Relationship Must Know +

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) is the most common measure.

Pearson Correlation Coefficient
r = Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)] / √[Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² × Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)²]

Range: −1 ≤ r ≤ +1

r = +1: Perfect positive linear relationship
r = −1: Perfect negative linear relationship
r = 0: No linear relationship (may have non-linear!)
r = +0.67: Strong positive relationship (ice cream & temperature)
⚠️ CORRELATION ≠ CAUSATION: Ice cream sales and drowning incidents are highly correlated (both increase in summer) but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. The hidden variable is summer heat. This is a classic example of spurious correlation.
💼 Business Applications
  • Marketing: Correlation between advertising spend and sales revenue
  • Finance: Portfolio diversification — stocks with low/negative correlation reduce risk
  • HR: Correlation between training hours and employee performance
  • Supply Chain: Correlation between temperature and demand for cold beverages
Regression — Predicting One Variable from Another Business Critical +

While correlation measures relationship strength, regression finds the best-fit line to predict Y from X (or multiple Xs in multiple regression).

Simple Linear Regression
Y = a + bX + ε

b (slope/regression coefficient) = r × (σy/σx)
a (intercept) = ȳ − b·x̄

R² (coefficient of determination) = r²
R² = % of variation in Y explained by X
If R² = 0.85 → X explains 85% of Y's variation
💼 Business Applications
  • Predicting next quarter's sales based on ad spend (linear regression)
  • Real estate: predicting house price from square footage
  • Forecasting demand based on price, season, competition
  • IIM Sirmaur asked: "Use a statistical tool to predict sales for next quarter" — answer is regression

Index Numbers

Measuring relative changes over time — prices, quantities, economy

An index number is a ratio that measures the relative change in a variable (or group of variables) with respect to a base period. The base period index = 100.

Simple (Unweighted) Index

Price Relative = (Pₙ / P₀) × 100
where Pₙ = current price, P₀ = base price

Laspeyres Index (Base-weighted)

Uses base period quantities as weights.
Pₗ = Σ(Pₙ·Q₀) / Σ(P₀·Q₀) × 100
Tends to overestimate inflation.

Paasche Index (Current-weighted)

Uses current period quantities as weights.
Pₚ = Σ(Pₙ·Qₙ) / Σ(P₀·Qₙ) × 100
Tends to underestimate inflation.

Fisher's Ideal Index

Geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche.
F = √(Laspeyres × Paasche)
Called "ideal" as it satisfies Time Reversal & Factor Reversal tests.

Important Index Numbers in India
WPI — Wholesale Price Index (measures inflation at wholesale level)
CPI — Consumer Price Index (measures retail inflation; RBI targets ~4%)
IIP — Index of Industrial Production
Sensex — Tracks 30 largest BSE-listed companies (base year 1978-79 = 100)
Nifty 50 — Tracks 50 largest NSE-listed companies (base 1995 = 1000)
💼 Business Applications
  • CPI is used by RBI to set repo rate (inflation targeting)
  • WPI used for business-to-business contract indexation
  • Sensex/Nifty measure overall stock market performance
  • Cost of Living Adjustment (COLA) in salary negotiations uses CPI
  • GDP deflator converts nominal GDP to real GDP

Hypothesis Testing & Inferential Statistics

Making decisions from data — the backbone of evidence-based management

Hypothesis Testing Framework Advanced +
The 5-Step Framework
1. State H₀ (Null) and H₁ (Alternative) hypothesis
2. Choose significance level α (commonly 0.05)
3. Calculate test statistic (Z, t, χ², F)
4. Find p-value or compare with critical value
5. Decision: Reject H₀ if p-value < α

Null Hypothesis (H₀)

The status quo / no effect. "The new drug has no effect." "Mean = 50." We never "prove" H₀; we either reject it or fail to reject it.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)

What we want to prove. "The new drug works." "Mean ≠ 50." Rejecting H₀ provides evidence for H₁.

Type I Error (α)

Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true. "False positive." Significance level α controls this. Convicting an innocent person.

Type II Error (β)

Failing to reject H₀ when it is false. "False negative." Letting a guilty person go free. Power = 1−β.

p-value
p-value = P(observing this data | H₀ is true)

Small p-value → data is unlikely under H₀ → evidence against H₀
p < 0.05 → statistically significant at 5% level → reject H₀
💼 Business Applications
  • A/B Testing: "Does the new website design increase conversion rate?" H₀: no difference. If p < 0.05, launch the new design.
  • Quality Control: Is the mean weight of cereal boxes significantly different from stated 500g?
  • Marketing: Did the new ad campaign significantly increase sales?
  • HR: Is there a statistically significant gender pay gap in this company?
Central Limit Theorem (CLT) — The Most Important Theorem in Statistics Fundamental +

The CLT states that the distribution of sample means approaches a normal distribution as sample size n increases, regardless of the shape of the population distribution.

Central Limit Theorem
If X₁, X₂,...,Xₙ are i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ²,
then as n → ∞:

x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n) approximately

Standard Error of Mean = σ/√n

Rule of thumb: n ≥ 30 is usually sufficient

Why it matters: Even if individual data points follow a skewed or bizarre distribution, the average of many samples follows a normal distribution. This is why we can apply normal-distribution-based tests to almost any dataset with large enough samples.

💼 Business Applications
  • Quality control: sample means of product weights follow normal distribution even if individual weights don't
  • Polling: average opinion across thousands of people follows normal distribution
  • Finance: portfolio returns (average of many stocks) approach normality
  • Risk management: CLT underlies most statistical risk models

  • 34
    What is the null hypothesis? What does "rejecting the null hypothesis" mean?
    IIM Ranchi (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    The null hypothesis (H₀) represents the default assumption — that there is no effect, no difference, or no relationship. It's what we assume is true unless data provides strong evidence against it.

    "Rejecting H₀" means we found statistically significant evidence that H₀ is false. Example: H₀ = "The new drug has no effect." If our clinical trial data yields p = 0.02 (less than α = 0.05), we reject H₀ and conclude the drug does have a significant effect.

    Important: Rejecting H₀ doesn't mean proving H₁ with certainty — it means the probability of seeing this data if H₀ were true is very low (less than 5%).
  • 35
    What is p-value? How is it related to alpha (significance level)?
    IIM Ranchi (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    The p-value is the probability of observing results as extreme as (or more extreme than) the sample data, assuming H₀ is true. A small p-value means the data is very unlikely under H₀, providing evidence against it.

    Alpha (α) is the significance level — our predetermined threshold for "small enough." Typically α = 0.05 (5%).

    Decision rule: If p-value < α → reject H₀ (statistically significant result).
    If p-value ≥ α → fail to reject H₀ (insufficient evidence).

    Business translation: p = 0.03 with α = 0.05 → "We're 97% confident this result isn't due to chance. Launch the new strategy."

    IIM Kozhikode
    ✅ Model Answer
    CLT states: regardless of the population's original distribution, as you take larger and larger samples (n ≥ 30 typically), the distribution of sample means approaches a normal distribution with mean = population mean and SD = population SD / √n.

    This is revolutionary because: (1) We can use normal distribution tools on almost any real-world data if we take large enough samples. (2) It explains why the normal distribution appears everywhere in nature — most natural measurements are averages of many random factors. (3) It's the basis for confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and much of applied statistics.

Bonus Concepts & Quick Reference

Sampling, law of large numbers, data types, and more often-tested ideas

Law of Large Numbers & Sampling +

Law of Large Numbers

As the number of trials increases, the sample mean converges to the population mean. Example: toss a fair coin 10 times — you might get 7 heads. Toss 10,000 times — heads will be very close to 5,000. This underpins insurance and casino profitability.

Random vs Stratified Sampling

Random: Every unit has equal chance of selection. Simple, unbiased.
Stratified: Divide population into strata (groups), sample from each. Better representation. Used in national surveys.

Quartiles, Percentiles & Box Plot +
Quartiles
Q1 = 25th percentile (lower quartile)
Q2 = 50th percentile = Median
Q3 = 75th percentile (upper quartile)
IQR = Q3 − Q1 (Interquartile Range)

Outlier thresholds: Below Q1−1.5×IQR or Above Q3+1.5×IQR
💼 Business Applications
  • IIM salary reports: median, P25 (Q1), P75 (Q3) give the full picture
  • Box plots in quality control to visualise data spread and outliers
  • E-commerce: 90th percentile delivery time (P90) used for SLA commitments

    What is a random variable? What is the difference between discrete and continuous random variables?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    A random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment. It "translates" sample space into numbers.

    Discrete RV: Takes countable distinct values. Example: number of heads in 10 flips (0,1,2,...,10). Uses Probability Mass Function (PMF). Distributions: Binomial, Poisson, Geometric.

    Continuous RV: Takes any value in a range (uncountably infinite). Example: height, temperature, stock price. Uses Probability Density Function (PDF). P(X = exact value) = 0. We calculate P(a < X < b) using area under curve. Distributions: Normal, Uniform, Exponential.

    What is normal distribution? What is standard normal distribution?
    IIM Shillong (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Normal distribution: symmetric bell-shaped curve defined by mean μ and variance σ². Denoted N(μ, σ²).

    Standard Normal Distribution: special case where μ=0 and σ=1. Denoted Z ~ N(0,1). Any normal distribution can be converted to standard normal using z-score: Z = (X−μ)/σ.

    Standard normal has z-tables that give P(Z < z) for any z value. Properties: total area = 1, symmetric about 0, P(Z < 0) = 0.5, P(−1 < Z < 1) ≈ 0.68.
  • 41
    What is the difference between parameter and statistic?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    A parameter is a numerical characteristic of the entire population. It is fixed (though usually unknown). Denoted by Greek letters: μ (mean), σ (SD), ρ (correlation).

    A statistic is a numerical characteristic calculated from a sample. It varies from sample to sample (sampling variability). Denoted by Roman letters: x̄ (sample mean), s (sample SD), r (sample correlation).

    We use statistics to estimate parameters. The sample mean x̄ is our best estimate of population mean μ.

    What is t-value/t-test? When do you use it?
    IIM Indore (actual, inferential statistics)
    ✅ Model Answer
    The t-test is used to determine if the means of two groups are statistically significantly different, or if a sample mean differs significantly from a known value. We use it when: (1) sample size is small (n < 30), (2) population standard deviation is unknown (we use sample SD instead).

    t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

    The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal distribution, accounting for extra uncertainty from using sample SD. As n → ∞, t-distribution → normal distribution. Business use: testing if the average customer satisfaction score changed after a service redesign.
  • 43
    Why is SD squared when calculating variance? Why not just take the absolute value of deviations?
    IIM Amritsar · Ranchi
    ✅ Model Answer
    Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) does exist and uses absolute values. We prefer squaring because: (1) Mathematical properties: Squared functions are differentiable everywhere; absolute value is not differentiable at 0, making optimisation harder. (2) Additivity: Variances of independent variables add up (Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y) if independent). This doesn't hold for MAD. (3) Penalising outliers: Squaring penalises large deviations more than proportionally — a deviation of 4 contributes 16, while deviation of 2 contributes 4 (4× the contribution for 2× the deviation). This is often desirable in risk management.
  • 44
    What is normalisation in statistics?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    Normalisation (or standardisation) is transforming data to a common scale without distorting differences in ranges. Two common methods:

    1. Z-score standardisation: Z = (X−μ)/σ → transforms to mean=0, SD=1. Used in hypothesis testing, machine learning.
    2. Min-Max normalisation: X' = (X−Xmin)/(Xmax−Xmin) → scales to [0,1] range.

    Used in: comparing datasets with different units (e.g., comparing income in ₹ with age in years for regression), machine learning feature scaling, creating composite indices.
  • 45
    If I toss a coin 10 times and get heads all 10 times, what is the probability of getting a head on the 11th toss?
    IIM Amritsar (actual)
    ✅ Model Answer
    1/2 — assuming the coin is fair. Coin tosses are independent events. The coin has no memory. Past results do not affect future probabilities. This is called the Gambler's Fallacy — the mistaken belief that after a run of heads, tails is "due."

    However, if we use Bayesian thinking: 10 heads in a row makes us question whether the coin IS fair (posterior probability of p=0.5 decreases, probability of biased coin increases). This Bayesian update would change our estimate of the 11th toss probability.
