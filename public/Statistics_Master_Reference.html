Data Fundamentals
🗂️
Data Types
Qualitative vs Quantitative · Discrete vs Continuous
▼
Data is a collection of facts, figures, or information gathered for analysis. All data falls into one of two broad types: Qualitative (descriptive, non-numeric) or Quantitative (numeric, measurable).
  • Nominal: No order — gender, blood type, city name
  • Ordinal: Has order, no equal gaps — ratings (1-5 stars), education level
  • Discrete: Countable, whole numbers — no. of customers, goals scored
  • Continuous: Any value in range — weight, temperature, revenue
📊 Business Use: A shoe store's inventory uses discrete data (pairs of shoes). Sales revenue uses continuous data. Customer satisfaction surveys produce ordinal data.
IIM Amritsar
What is the difference between data types?
Sir, data is broadly qualitative (descriptive attributes like brand loyalty or customer gender) or quantitative (measurable like revenue or weight). Quantitative data is further discrete — countable integers like number of transactions — or continuous like a stock price that can take any decimal value. Crucially, the type of data determines which statistical tool to apply.
👥
Population vs Sample · Census vs Sampling
The fundamental distinction in statistics
▼
The entire group you want to draw conclusions about. E.g., all 140 crore Indians, all NSE-listed companies.
A subset selected from the population to represent it. E.g., 1,000 voters surveyed before elections.
Collecting data from every member of the population. India's census is done every 10 years. Expensive, time-consuming but complete.
Studying a representative subset to infer about the whole. Cost-effective, faster, but subject to sampling error.
Population Mean = μ (mu) | Sample Mean = x̄ (x-bar) Population SD = σ (sigma) | Sample SD = s Population size = N | Sample size = n
📊 Business Use: FMCG companies like HUL use sampling to test a new shampoo formulation on 500 consumers before launching to 100 crore. Netflix samples viewing behaviour of 1% of users to adjust recommendations for all.
Measures of Central Tendency
📍
Mean, Median, Mode
The three most-asked stats concepts in IIM interviews
▼
⚡ HOT: Asked in IIM Amritsar, Ranchi, Kozhikode, Shillong — "Define mean, median, mode and give real-world application"
Sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. Most commonly used but sensitive to extreme values (outliers).
Mean (x̄) = (Σxᵢ) / n Example: Data: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 → Mean = 150/5 = 30
The middle value when data is arranged in order. For even number of values, average the two middle values. Not affected by outliers.
Odd n: Median = ((n+1)/2)th value Even n: Median = average of (n/2)th and (n/2+1)th values Example: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 → Median = 30
The value that appears most frequently. A dataset can be unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal. The only measure used for categorical data.
📊 Why we need all three:
  • Mean: Average salary at a company. But if 1 CEO earns ₹10 Cr and 99 employees earn ₹5L, mean is misleading.
  • Median: Better for salaries, house prices (skewed data). India's per capita income uses mean but median gives a truer picture.
  • Mode: Most popular shoe size in a store (decides which size to stock most).
IIM Amritsar — Panel 2
Give a business example of using mean, median and mode.
Sir, imagine a garment store. The mode tells which size (M, L, XL) sells most — so we stock that size more. The median gives the middle price point customers actually pay, unaffected if a few buy very expensive items. The mean helps calculate average daily revenue to plan monthly targets. X-Factor: When McKinsey reports "average CEO pay," critics rightly say we should look at the median because a few billionaires skew the mean upward — exactly why all three measures matter.
IIM Amritsar
If the mean is 50 and median is 45, how is the data skewed?
Sir, when Mean > Median, the distribution is positively skewed (right skewed). This means there are some very large values pulling the mean up. The tail extends to the right. Example: Income distribution in India is right-skewed — a few billionaires pull the average up while the median income is much lower.
⚖️
Weighted Mean · Geometric Mean · Harmonic Mean
Advanced averages for specific situations
▼
Average where each value has a different importance (weight). Used when not all observations contribute equally.
Weighted Mean = Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / Σwᵢ Example: Exam weights — Quiz 20%, Mid 30%, Final 50% Scores: 80, 70, 90 → WM = (0.2×80 + 0.3×70 + 0.5×90) = 82
📊 Use: Stock index calculation (BSE Sensex is market-cap weighted), GPA calculation, portfolio returns.
The nth root of the product of n values. Best for rates of change, growth rates, and ratios. Always ≤ Arithmetic Mean.
GM = (x₁ × x₂ × ... × xₙ)^(1/n) Example: Growth rates: 10%, 20%, 30% GM = (1.1 × 1.2 × 1.3)^(1/3) - 1 ≈ 19.7%
📊 Use: CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) uses GM. India's GDP CAGR of ~7% over a decade is calculated using GM.
Reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of reciprocals. Best for rates and speeds.
HM = n / Σ(1/xᵢ) Example: Speeds 60 km/h and 40 km/h for equal distances HM = 2/(1/60 + 1/40) = 48 km/h (not 50!)
📊 Use: P/E ratio averaging in finance. Average speed calculations. Always: HM ≤ GM ≤ AM.
Measures of Dispersion
📐
Range · Variance · Standard Deviation · CV
How spread out is the data?
▼
⚡ HOT: "What is standard deviation? Explain to a layman." — Asked at IIM Amritsar, Ranchi (multiple panels)
Range = Maximum value − Minimum value Simple but ignores all values in between. Very sensitive to outliers.
Average of squared deviations from the mean. Squaring ensures negative deviations don't cancel positive ones, and penalises large deviations.
Population Variance: σ² = Σ(xᵢ - μ)² / N Sample Variance: s² = Σ(xᵢ - x̄)² / (n-1) [Bessel's correction] Why n-1? → Makes sample variance an unbiased estimator of population variance
Square root of variance. Expressed in the same units as the original data — this is why we prefer SD over variance in practice.
σ = √Variance If mean = 10, SD = 2 → Most data falls between 8 and 12 (within 1 SD) 68% of data lies within ±1σ of mean (for normal distribution)
Relative measure of dispersion. Expresses SD as a percentage of mean. Used to compare variability across different scales.
CV = (σ / μ) × 100 If Stock A: Mean=₹100, SD=₹10 → CV=10% If Stock B: Mean=₹10, SD=₹2 → CV=20% → Stock B is relatively MORE volatile despite smaller absolute SD
📊 Business Use: Risk in finance is measured by SD of returns. Mutual fund factsheets show SD. The Sharpe Ratio compares return per unit of SD. In manufacturing, Six Sigma aims to reduce SD to bring defects below 3.4 per million.
IIM Amritsar — Panel 4
What is standard deviation? Explain it to a layman.
Sir, standard deviation tells us how much individual values typically deviate from the average. Imagine 5 students score 10, 10, 10, 10, 10 in a test — SD is 0, everyone got the same. But if scores are 2, 6, 10, 14, 18 — average is still 10 but SD is 6, meaning students are very spread out. X-Factor: India's per capita income mean is ₹1.75 lakh, but the high SD reveals massive inequality — a Mukesh Ambani skews the mean enormously. The SD exposes what the mean hides.
IIM Amritsar
Why do we need standard deviation if we already have variance?
Sir, variance is in squared units — if we measure weight in kg, variance is in kg². Standard deviation, as the square root of variance, brings us back to the original unit (kg), making it interpretable. If mean salary is ₹50,000 and variance is ₹25,00,00,000 — that number means nothing. SD = ₹5,000 is immediately interpretable: most people earn within ₹5,000 of the average.

📦
Quartiles · Percentiles · Deciles
Positional measures of data
▼
Quartiles divide data into 4 equal parts. Deciles into 10 equal parts. Percentiles into 100 equal parts.
Q1 (25th percentile) = Lower quartile — 25% of data falls below Q2 (50th percentile) = Median — 50% below Q3 (75th percentile) = Upper quartile — 75% below IQR (Interquartile Range) = Q3 - Q1 [most important!] Outlier rule: values < Q1 - 1.5×IQR or > Q3 + 1.5×IQR
📊 Business Use: IPMAT percentile (99th percentile = top 1%). JEE rank. Income percentile. Box plots use IQR. NITI Aayog uses poverty headcount using percentile thresholds.
IIM Amritsar
What is the difference between percentage and percentile?
Sir, percentage is an absolute measure — it tells you your score out of 100 (you scored 85%). Percentile is a relative measure — it tells your position relative to others (85th percentile means you scored better than 85% of all test-takers). IPMAT score of 200 might be 99th percentile — your marks are absolute, but percentile shows where you stand in the crowd.
Correlation, Covariance & Regression
🔗
Correlation & Covariance
Relationship between two variables
▼
⚡ HOT: Asked at IIM Amritsar Panel 3 & 6 — "What is correlation? Give a business example."
Measures how two variables change together. Sign tells direction, but magnitude is hard to interpret (depends on units).
Cov(X,Y) = Σ[(xᵢ - x̄)(yᵢ - ȳ)] / (n-1) Positive: both move in same direction Negative: they move in opposite directions
Standardised covariance. Always between -1 and +1. Unitless, comparable across datasets.
r = Cov(X,Y) / (σₓ × σᵧ) r = +1 → Perfect positive correlation r = 0 → No linear relationship r = -1 → Perfect negative correlation r = 0.67 → Moderate positive (e.g., ice cream sales & temperature)
📊 Business Examples:
  • Temperature & ice cream sales: Strong positive correlation (+0.8)
  • Interest rates & bond prices: Strong negative (-0.9)
  • Portfolio diversification: Choose stocks with low/negative correlation to reduce risk
  • Marketing spend & revenue: Should show positive r
IIM Amritsar — Panel 3
If ice cream sales increase in summer, is it correlation? What value of r would it have?
Yes sir, it's a positive correlation — both temperature and ice cream sales rise together, so r would be positive. Among -0.5, 0, and 0.67, I would estimate r = +0.67, indicating a moderate to strong positive relationship. The caveat, however, is that this is correlation, not causation — the actual cause is temperature, not ice cream causing summer!
📈
Regression
Predicting one variable from another
▼
Regression models the relationship between a dependent variable (Y) and one or more independent variables (X). Simple linear regression fits a straight line through data points.
Y = a + bX + ε Where: a = intercept (Y when X=0) b = regression coefficient (slope — change in Y per unit X) ε = error term (residual) b = Cov(X,Y) / Var(X) = r × (σᵧ/σₓ)
📊 Business Use: Sales forecasting (predict Q4 sales based on ad spend). Real estate pricing (predict flat price from area, location). Netflix predicts rating a user would give a movie.
IIM Shillong
What do you understand by regression? What is regression coefficient?
Sir, regression is a statistical tool that finds the best-fit relationship between variables to make predictions. In simple linear regression Y = a + bX, the coefficient 'b' tells us by how much Y changes when X increases by 1 unit. If b = 3 for sales vs. advertising spend, every extra ₹1 lakh in ads generates ₹3 lakh more sales. Amazon uses regression with hundreds of variables to predict demand and pre-position products in warehouses before you even click "buy."
Probability
🎲
Probability Fundamentals
Classical, relative frequency, and axiomatic
▼
⚡ HOT: Dice problems asked across IIM Amritsar, Shillong, Ranchi, Sirmaur, Kozhikode multiple times!
Probability is the measure of the likelihood that an event will occur. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
P(A) = Favourable outcomes / Total equally likely outcomes P(A) + P(A') = 1 [complement rule] 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
Event TypeDefinitionExample
Mutually ExclusiveCannot occur together: P(A∩B) = 0Head and Tail on same coin toss
IndependentOne doesn't affect the other: P(A∩B) = P(A)×P(B)Two separate coin tosses
ExhaustiveCover all possibilities{H, T} for a coin toss
ComplementaryP(A) + P(A') = 1Rain or No Rain
Two dice: P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 [most common!] Two dice: P(sum = 9) = 4/36 = 1/9 → (3,6),(4,5),(5,4),(6,3) Face card from 52: P = 12/52 = 3/13 53 Sundays in a year (365 days): 365 = 52×7 + 1 extra day → P(1 specific day) = 1/7 Leap year (366 days): 366 = 52×7 + 2 extra days → P(Sunday in extra 2) = 2/7
IIM Shillong
If two dice are cast, what is the probability of getting sum of 9?
Sir, total outcomes with 2 dice = 6×6 = 36. For sum = 9, favourable pairs are: (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) — that's 4 outcomes. So P(sum=9) = 4/36 = 1/9 ≈ 0.111.
IIM Amritsar
If I toss a coin and get 10 heads on 10 flips, what is the probability of head on the 11th flip?
Sir, coin tosses are independent events. The probability of heads on any single toss is always 1/2, regardless of previous outcomes. Past results have no memory effect on future independent events. This is called the Gambler's Fallacy when people mistakenly believe otherwise.
🔄
Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem
Updating probability with new information
▼
⚡ HOT: Asked at IIM Amritsar (multiple panels), IIM Kozhikode, IIM Ranchi Mumbai
The probability of event A occurring given that event B has already occurred. Written P(A|B).
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) Example: P(Ace | Red card) = P(Red Ace)/P(Red) = (2/52)/(26/52) = 2/26 = 1/13
A way to reverse conditional probability — update your prior belief based on new evidence.
P(A|B) = [P(B|A) × P(A)] / P(B) Where: P(A) = Prior probability (belief before evidence) P(B|A) = Likelihood (probability of evidence given hypothesis) P(A|B) = Posterior probability (updated belief)
📊 Business Examples:
  • Medical Testing: Given a positive COVID test result, what's the actual probability of having COVID? (depends on disease prevalence and test accuracy)
  • Spam Filters: Gmail uses Bayes to classify emails — P(spam|word "free") based on historical data
  • Insurance: Updating risk assessment when a driver has an accident
IIM Amritsar — Panel 1
Explain Bayes' Theorem with an example.
Sir, Bayes' theorem allows us to update our probability estimates when we receive new evidence. Classic example: 1% of population has a disease. Test is 99% accurate. If someone tests positive, what's the probability they truly have the disease? Intuitively we say 99%, but Bayes shows it's actually only ~50%. P(disease|positive) = P(positive|disease) × P(disease) / P(positive). X-Factor: This is exactly why mass COVID testing required careful interpretation — a positive test on a low-prevalence population had high false-positive risk.
🎯
Expected Value · Random Variables
Quantifying uncertainty
▼
A variable whose value is determined by a random experiment. Can be discrete (countable outcomes) or continuous (uncountable range).
Discrete: X = number of heads in 3 coin tosses {0, 1, 2, 3} Continuous: X = time taken to complete a task (any positive real value)
The long-run average value of a random variable. Weighted average of all possible outcomes.
E[X] = Σ [xᵢ × P(xᵢ)] Example: Fair die → E[X] = 1×(1/6) + 2×(1/6) + ... + 6×(1/6) = 3.5 Note: 3.5 is not a possible outcome, but it's the average over many rolls!
📊 Business Use: Insurance premium pricing (expected payout), investment return calculations, casino game design, product warranty pricing. An insurance company prices premiums using expected claims to remain profitable.
Probability Distributions
🔔
Binomial · Poisson · Normal Distribution
The three most important distributions
▼
⚡ HOT: IIM Amritsar asked "formula of Poisson and Binomial distribution" and "toss coin 5 times, probability of not winning"
Models number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, each with probability p of success.
P(X = k) = ⁿCₖ × pᵏ × (1-p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ Mean = np | Variance = np(1-p) | SD = √[np(1-p)] Conditions: Fixed n, 2 outcomes (S/F), constant p, independent trials Example: Prob of exactly 3 heads in 5 tosses: P(3) = ⁵C₃ × (0.5)³ × (0.5)² = 10 × 0.125 × 0.25 = 0.3125
📊 Use: Quality control (defective items), election polls, drug trials (patients who respond to treatment).
Models the number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time/space, when events occur at a known average rate (λ) independently.
P(X = k) = (e⁻λ × λᵏ) / k! Mean = λ | Variance = λ | (Mean = Variance — unique property!) Example: If average 3 customers arrive per minute (λ=3), P(exactly 5 arrive) = (e⁻³ × 3⁵) / 5! = 0.101
📊 Use: Call centre traffic modelling, server request arrivals, insurance claims per year, number of typos per page.
The famous "bell curve". Symmetric, continuous distribution defined entirely by its mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ). Most real-world phenomena approximate it.
f(x) = (1/σ√2π) × e^[-(x-μ)²/2σ²] 68-95-99.7 RULE: • 68% of data within μ ± 1σ • 95% of data within μ ± 2σ • 99.7% of data within μ ± 3σ Standard Normal: Z = (X - μ) / σ [mean=0, SD=1]
IIM Shillong
Questions on normal distribution and standard normal distribution.
Sir, the normal distribution is a symmetric bell-shaped curve where mean = median = mode. It's defined by μ and σ. The standard normal distribution is a special case where μ=0 and σ=1, obtained by Z-standardisation: Z = (X-μ)/σ. This Z-score tells us how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. X-Factor: Bell curve with SD is how symmetric and SD are related — a narrow bell means low SD and high consistency, a flat bell means high SD and more variability in outcomes.
⚡
Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
The most powerful theorem in statistics
▼
CLT: As sample size (n) increases, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution, regardless of the shape of the population distribution — provided n ≥ 30.
Sample Mean Distribution: Mean = μ (same as population) Standard Error = σ/√n (decreases as n increases) → Larger sample = sampling distribution more tightly clustered around μ
📊 Why it's powerful: It justifies using normal distribution-based inference tools (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) even when the underlying data isn't normal. This is why statistics works across nearly all fields.
💡 Analogy: Ask 5 random people India's average salary — wildly varying. Ask 5,000 — your average estimate converges to the truth. CLT is why surveys of 1,000 people can predict an election of 100 crore voters.
Sampling, Confidence Intervals & Hypothesis Testing
🔬
Sampling Methods & Sampling Error
How to collect data correctly
▼
MethodDescriptionUse Case
Simple RandomEvery member has equal chance of selectionLottery draws, basic surveys
StratifiedDivide into strata, sample from each proportionallyGender-balanced surveys
SystematicSelect every kth member from a listFactory quality check (every 10th unit)
ClusterRandomly select entire groupsVillage surveys, schools

Sampling Error: The difference between sample statistic and true population parameter, arising purely from chance in selecting the sample. Reduces as sample size increases.
📊 Business: IMRB and Nielsen use stratified sampling for market research. Political polls use quota sampling. Drug trials use randomized controlled trials (simple random).
📏
Confidence Intervals
Range estimate for a population parameter
▼
A confidence interval (CI) is a range of values, derived from sample data, that is likely to contain the true population parameter at a specified confidence level.
CI = x̄ ± (Z × σ/√n) For 95% CI: Z = 1.96 For 99% CI: Z = 2.576 Example: Sample mean = 50, SD = 10, n = 100 95% CI = 50 ± 1.96×(10/√100) = 50 ± 1.96 = [48.04, 51.96]
⚠️ Common Mistake: "95% CI" does NOT mean "95% probability the true mean is in this interval." It means: if we repeated the sampling 100 times, ~95 of the intervals would contain the true mean.
📊 Use: Drug efficacy studies ("Blood pressure reduced by 10-15 mmHg with 95% confidence"). Polling ("BJP leads with 42% ± 3% margin of error").
⚖️
Hypothesis Testing · p-value · Type I & II Errors
Making decisions with data
▼
⚡ HOT: IIM Indore asked about "null hypothesis," "p-value," and "Type 1 error" in a single interview!
H₀ (Null Hypothesis): Default assumption (e.g., new drug has NO effect) H�� (Alternate Hypothesis): What we want to prove (drug HAS effect) α (Significance Level): Usually 0.05 (5%) — threshold for rejecting H₀ Reject H₀ if: test statistic > critical value, OR p-value < α
The probability of observing results at least as extreme as those obtained, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value means the null hypothesis is unlikely to be true.
p-value < 0.05 → Reject H₀ (statistically significant result) p-value > 0.05 → Fail to reject H₀ (insufficient evidence) p-value = 0.03 means: If H₀ were true, only 3% chance of seeing these results
H₀ TrueH₀ False
Reject H₀Type I Error (α) — False Positive✅ Correct Decision (Power)
Fail to Reject✅ Correct (1-α)Type II Error (β) — False Negative
📊 Real-world consequences:
  • Type I (False Positive): Approving a drug that doesn't work — wasted money, potential harm
  • Type II (False Negative): Rejecting a drug that works — patients denied effective treatment
  • Spam filter: Type I = marking real email as spam; Type II = letting spam through
IIM Indore
What is null hypothesis? What is p-value? What is Type I error?
Sir, the null hypothesis is our starting assumption — that there is no effect or difference. We try to disprove it with data. The p-value is the probability of seeing our observed results (or more extreme) if the null hypothesis were true — a p-value of 0.03 means just a 3% chance, so we reject H₀. A Type I error is rejecting a true null hypothesis — a false positive. In drug testing, it's approving an ineffective drug. X-Factor: This is why FDA requires p < 0.05 for drug approval — to keep the false discovery rate low.
Statistical Pitfalls & Biases
🧠
Outliers · Survivorship Bias · Correlation vs Causation
The traps that fool analysts and managers
▼
Data points that lie significantly far from other observations. Can indicate errors, genuine rare events, or important signals.
Detection: Values beyond Q1 - 1.5×IQR or Q3 + 1.5×IQR are outliers Also: Z-score > ±3 indicates an outlier (3 SDs from mean)
📊 Impact: Outliers can massively distort the mean. Ratan Tata in a room of 99 average people raises mean salary enormously — use median instead for such data.
The logical error of focusing only on entities that "survived" some process and ignoring those that didn't, leading to incorrect conclusions.
💡 Classic Example: WWII engineers studied bullet holes on returning planes and reinforced those areas. Abraham Wald (statistician) said: reinforce the areas with NO holes — the planes hit there didn't return! The survivors show where you CAN take damage; the missing planes show where you can't.
📊 Business Examples:
  • Mutual Funds: Magazines list "Top 10 funds over 10 years" — but hundreds of underperforming funds were quietly shut down. The top list has survivorship bias.
  • Startups: "Entrepreneurs are college dropouts" — Bill Gates, Zuckerberg are visible. Thousands of dropouts who failed are invisible.
  • Books: "Successful habits of rich people" — we only study the rich, not the equally hard-working people who didn't become rich.
Two variables can be correlated without one causing the other. Always ask: Is there a confounding variable? Is it reverse causation?
Spurious CorrelationWhat Actually Explains It
Ice cream sales & drowning deathsBoth caused by summer heat (confounder)
Shoe size & reading ability in childrenBoth caused by age
Nicolas Cage films & pool drownings (famous dataset!)Pure coincidence, no mechanism
📊 Critical Thinking: "Revenue increased after we changed our logo" — was it the logo or the new product line launched simultaneously? Always look for A/B testing (randomized controlled experiments) to establish causation, not just correlation.
Conceptual
How is probability used as a tool in business?
Sir, probability is the backbone of business decision-making under uncertainty. Insurance companies price premiums using expected loss probabilities. Banks use credit default probabilities in lending decisions. Operations teams use queue theory (Poisson distribution) to staff call centres optimally. Marketing teams use A/B testing — if 10% more users click a new button vs. old button with p-value = 0.02, they roll it out company-wide. X-Factor: Amazon uses hundreds of simultaneous A/B tests — probability-based — to optimise everything from button colour to recommendation algorithms.
Time Series & Index Numbers

Time Series Analysis
Patterns in data over time
▼
A time series is a sequence of data points measured at successive time intervals. It has four components.
ComponentDescriptionExample
Trend (T)Long-term directionIndia's GDP growing over decades
Seasonal (S)Repeating short-term patternsIce cream peaks every summer
Cyclical (C)Long-term economic cycles (>1 year)Business cycle: boom → recession
Irregular (I)Random, unpredictable fluctuationsCOVID impact, natural disasters
Additive Model: Y = T + S + C + I Multiplicative Model: Y = T × S × C × I CAGR = [(End Value / Start Value)^(1/n) - 1] × 100%
📊 Business Use: Demand forecasting, stock price analysis, economic indicators like CPI and GDP. Netflix predicts viewership peaks (Friday evenings, holiday seasons) using seasonal decomposition to scale servers.

Index Numbers
Measuring change relative to a base period
▼
An index number is a statistical measure that shows how a value has changed relative to a chosen base period (set at 100).
Simple Price Index = (Current Price / Base Price) × 100 If wheat was ₹20/kg in 2015 (base) and ₹30 in 2023: Index = (30/20) × 100 = 150 → 50% increase

📊 X-Factor: India's Gini coefficient (~35) measures income inequality — 0 = perfect equality, 1 = one person owns everything. Compares with Nordic countries (~25) vs. South Africa (~63). RBI's CPI target of 4% ± 2% governs all interest rate decisions.

IIM Ranchi Mumbai
Explain mean, median, mode with a real-life example. Where are they used?
Sir, consider test scores: 45, 55, 60, 60, 75, 80, 95. Mean = 67.1, Median = 60, Mode = 60. Mean gives the class average for report cards. Median is used by real estate sites to show "typical" house price, unaffected by a ₹100Cr bungalow. Mode helps a teacher know which score students most commonly achieve, indicating where to focus teaching. India's per capita income uses mean — ₹1.7L — but the median Indian earns far less, revealing inequality that the mean hides.
IIM Kozhikode
Questions on correlation, random variable, and normal distribution.
Sir, correlation measures the direction and strength of relationship between two variables, ranging from -1 to +1. A random variable is a variable whose value is determined by a probability experiment — discrete if countable (dice outcomes), continuous if any value in a range (stock price). Normal distribution is the bell-shaped curve described by mean μ and SD σ, where 68% of data lies within ±1σ. Height distribution in India is approximately normal — most people cluster around 5'5"-5'7", with few very tall or very short people.

Why is standard deviation preferred over variance?
Sir, variance is in squared units — if measuring sales in ₹, variance is in ₹². This is uninterpretable. Standard deviation, as its square root, is in the original unit (₹), making it directly comparable to the mean. If mean salary = ₹50,000 and SD = ₹10,000, you can immediately say most employees earn between ₹40,000–₹60,000. With variance of ₹1,00,000,000, that's meaningless for a manager.

What is a histogram? What is a frequency chart?
Sir, a frequency chart (or frequency distribution table) shows how many times each value or range of values occurs in a dataset. A histogram is its graphical representation — bars represent frequency of data falling in each class interval (a range, not a single value). Unlike a bar chart, histogram bars touch each other (no gaps) because the data is continuous. Frequency is a range of data points, not a single data point — the bar for "age 20-25" counts everyone in that range.

What is skewness? Draw graph of skewness and normal curve.
Sir, skewness measures asymmetry of a distribution. A positively skewed (right-skewed) distribution has a tail extending to the right — mean > median > mode. Common in income data. A negatively skewed (left-skewed) distribution has a tail to the left — mode > median > mean. Common in retirement age data. A symmetric (normal) distribution has zero skewness and all three measures coincide at the centre. Stock market returns are negatively skewed — small daily gains most of the time, punctuated by rare but severe crashes pulling the tail left.

What is AM-GM-HM relation?
Sir, for any set of positive numbers: HM ≤ GM ≤ AM, with equality only when all values are identical. Arithmetic Mean ≥ Geometric Mean ≥ Harmonic Mean. This is called the AM-GM-HM inequality. Practical example: speeds 60 and 40 km/h for equal distances — AM = 50, GM = √(60×40) = 48.99, HM = 48. The true average speed (HM) is 48 km/h, not 50.

What is CLT? What is the difference between parameter and statistics?
Sir, CLT states that the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches normal as sample size increases (n≥30), regardless of population shape. A parameter describes a population (fixed, often unknown) — e.g., population mean μ. A statistic is computed from a sample (varies from sample to sample) — e.g., sample mean x̄. We use statistics to estimate parameters. NSSO estimates India's unemployment rate (the population parameter) using survey statistics from 1 lakh households.
